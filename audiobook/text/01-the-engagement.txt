Episode One. The Engagement.

Forty-nine days to first snow.

* * *

Tyburn. The winter of fourteen eighty-nine.

There was no snow that night, only frost, thick as salt on the gallows field. A hundred empty frames stood in rows across the hill like the ribs of some animal that had died there and been picked clean. On one of them an old thief turned on his rope. Creak. Creak. Creak. Nobody had bothered to cut him down.

Below the field, in a pit lined with iron, something breathed.

The lanterns could not reach her face. They reached a hand, laid flat on the iron floor, a hand of blue ice as wide as a cartwheel, with a hook of black iron driven clean through the palm. And they reached the sound of her. She was singing. No words, only a tune, low and patient, the kind of tune a mother hums at a cradle when the child will not sleep and the night is long.

Alistair Harding stood over that hand in a cloak of wolf fur, a heavy man with a heavy jaw, frost in his beard, and on his finger a ring of old silver cut with a wolf's head. He drew a knife and opened the wrist of the thing in the pit. Her blood did not run red. It ran green, and it ran cold, into the mouth of a lantern held up beside him.

Fwhumph.

The lantern lit. A green-white flame, steady as a star.

The boy holding it was nine years old, small in a coat cut down from a man's, and his face in that light was solemn and proud, the face of a child who has been trusted with something heavy and means to carry it well. He held the lantern higher, so his father could see.

In the dark behind them, a tiny girl made of frost climbed out of the pit.

Alistair saw her. He looked at her for a long moment, and then he stepped aside and let her pass. He had what he wanted. The child ran barefoot into the white dark beyond the gallows, and from the trees came the long low howling of wolves, following her.

At the treeline a young woman stood in Harding colours. Nineteen, perhaps, a cousin of the house, her breath smoking, her hand pressed over her own mouth. She had seen all of it. She backed into the trees before anyone could see her face.

Under the frost, the singing went on.

* * *

Four hundred years later, London glowed green.

Every lamp on every street, every tram, every furnace, every chandelier in every great house from Mayfair to the river burned gallowglass, a pale crystal grown at the Harding Works on the old gallows field at Tyburn. The Hardings said it grew from iron in the ground. The city believed them, because the city was warm.

Under Gin Lane, in the lamp-tunnels the old Londoners called the Ring, the green was everywhere. It ran in glass mains along the brickwork, it pulsed in the junction boxes, it threw drowned-looking light on the wet walls. Through a grating overhead came the slap of a skipping rope and the voices of children chanting the oldest rhyme in London.

Green light, green light, who's in the cellar tonight?

Nobody down there listened.

Alice Bounds was crouched at a junction box with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a splicing knife in her teeth. She was twenty years old that day, small and quick and narrow as a blade, with dark hair scraped back hard under a man's flat cap, a face all cheekbone and suspicion, and grey eyes that did their sums before her mouth did anything. Her coat had been a soldier's once. Her boots had been two different people's. Her fingers were stained green to the second knuckle from years of running glim, the ground-up crystal the undercity smoked, snorted and sold.

"Watch this," she said around the knife.

Her sister Miriam leaned on the wall and did not watch, quite. Miriam Bounds was twenty-four, taller than Alice by a hand, with the same grey eyes set in a face that was easier to like. Her hair was a red-brown tangle pinned up anyhow, her mouth was always halfway to a joke, and her red wool mittens, the only bright thing either of them owned, were tucked into the pocket of a long brown coat. She was hard and she was quick and she was funny, and she was somewhere else tonight. Alice had noticed that hours ago.

Alice spliced the live line. A crackle, a smell like struck flint, and two streets west, a rival dealer's den went dark. Somewhere down the tunnel, faintly, a man swore.

Their buyer laughed. He was a long greasy streak of a man with a weeping eye and a glim pipe, and he had his head tilted strangely, listening. "Hear that? Crying. Always crying with this batch. Like a woman in the next room." He shrugged and pulled his belt loose to tie off a leaking pipe.

The buckle rang on the iron. Clink.

Alice was across the tunnel before she knew she had moved, back to the brick, breath stopped.

Miriam saw it. And Miriam looked away.

Then she was laughing, lifting an orange out of the buyer's coat pocket while he cursed and fumbled, and she tossed it underhand to Alice. "Catch, Ali." Alice caught it. The moment was gone, kicked under a rug like it always was.

It was Alice's birthday. She waited all through the deal for her sister to say so. Miriam didn't. She was touching something at her throat, under her collar: a ring, on a cord. Alice clocked it. She said nothing. She peeled the orange with her thumbnail and ate it, and it tasted of nothing at all.

* * *

Across the city, in the gauntlet shop of the Harding Works, a young man strapped a machine to his arm.

Nathaniel Harding was twenty-one, tall and loose-limbed, with a mop of dark curls he had never once tamed, ink on his cuffs, a burn scar on the back of one hand and a smear of grease along his jaw that he did not know was there. He wore his shirtsleeves rolled and a leather apron over a waistcoat that had cost more than most men on Gin Lane earned in a year, and he wore it badly, the way people do who think more about engines than mirrors. His eyes were his mother's, dark and watchful. His smile was entirely his own, and he did not use it often in his father's house.

The leap-gauntlet ran from his knuckles to his elbow, all brass ribs and steam valves, a pale crystal core humming in the forearm.

"Byron," he said. "Are you watching?"

"I am always watching, sir."

Byron, the Harding butler, stood by the door in a black tailcoat pressed to a knife's edge. He was sixty-four, straight-backed and silver-haired, his moustache clipped military-short, his face lined and patient and unreadable as a closed book. He had been a Lantern Guard sergeant once, long ago, and he still stood like one.

Nathaniel fired the booster.

Kshhh, THOOM.

He crossed the entire foundry in one jump, sailed over two workbenches and a startled apprentice, and came down feet-first in the coal bin. Whump. A black cloud rolled up to the rafters.

Byron walked over, unhurried, and picked a lump of soot out of the young man's hair with two gloved fingers. "Very graceful, sir. The coal agrees."

Nathaniel climbed out, black from boot to eyebrow, grinning like a boy. Byron had been the only audience for these demonstrations for years. Tonight, Nathaniel meant to have another. Tonight was the engagement ball, and his father would be there, and his father would see.

* * *

In the study of Harding House in Mayfair, a fire burned green behind a brass guard.

Eldgard Harding stood at the window with his hands clasped behind him. He was fifty-eight, broad through the chest and gone grey at the temples, his beard trimmed square, his evening coat black and perfect, a silver watch-chain across his waistcoat and on his right hand a heavy ring of old silver cut with a wolf's head. He had the stillness of a man who had decided long ago that he would not be moved by anything, and had mostly kept his word.

His elder son sat across the desk from him. Jamie Harding was twenty-five, fair where his brother was dark, lean and handsome and easy in his body, with a laugh that people remembered and a habit of looking at something just past your shoulder, as if the real conversation was happening there. Tonight he was not laughing.

"After tonight," Eldgard said, "you begin your preparation."

"I know what the Tithe is, Father."

Eldgard turned from the window. For a moment neither of them said anything. Then Eldgard poured two drinks from a decanter, and set one in front of his son.

Jamie didn't touch it. He stood, and on his way to the door he took out his pocket watch, wound it, and laid it on his father's desk, ticking, the way you might leave a thing behind if you didn't think you'd need to know the time where you were going.

Eldgard didn't notice it until much later.

Neither man said the word that mattered.

* * *

At the Lantern Guard tower at Charing Cross, under a ceiling painted with the corps' battle honours, a young man put his hand on a pane of green glass and swore an oath.

Nixon Hargrave was twenty-three, and the youngest Lantern-Captain in the history of the Royal Lantern Guard. He was built square and solid, a head shorter than Nathaniel and twice as steady, with close-cropped sandy hair, a jaw like a shut door and a broken nose that had healed slightly crooked in a charity-school fight. His dress uniform was bottle-green wool with brass frogging, a quench-blade of pale glass at his hip, and boots blacked until they reflected the lamps. He held himself like a man who knew exactly how far he had come and how easily he could be sent back.

"Light the dark," he said. "Keep the flame."

Eldgard Harding, his patron since the charity school, stepped forward and pinned a small green-glass lantern to his collar. "My family looks after its own."

Behind the captain, a Wick named Cole Pruett tried to snap off a salute. Cole was seventeen, all elbows and ears, with a thatch of straw-coloured hair, a face full of freckles and a uniform so new it still had the fold-creases in it. His salute missed his eyebrow entirely. He smacked himself in the eye.

Nobody laughed. Nixon very nearly did.

* * *

The Shadwell laundry was steam and lye and the slap of wet linen, a long brick hall by the river where forty women worked the coppers and the mangles and folded knives into the sheets.

Mother Grise ran it from a high chair by the counting table. She was seventy, small and bent and white-haired, wrapped in black shawls, her hands spotted and knotted as old rope, her eyes as pale and patient as a heron's. She ran the Vykra, the undercity's guild of women, spies and knives for hire, and she had outlived every enemy she had ever made by simply waiting.

She held out a sealed envelope.

"First contract," she said. "The client stays nameless. The mark is the second Harding son. Get close. Report everything."

Alice took it with both hands. She had wanted this for three years. Three years of laundry, of running errands, of being nobody's. Inside her own glove, she could feel the place where the Vykra knot would one day be stitched, if she earned it.

"There's a ball tonight," she said.

"Then you'd better get into it, hadn't you?"

* * *

At dusk Alice followed her sister.

She kept to the shadows along the river, the way the Vykra had taught her, down to the stair of Blackwood Bridge, a black iron span over the water with green lamps marching along its rail. She watched Miriam climb into a waiting coach. She watched a fair-haired young man lean out of it and pull her sister into a kiss.

Alice knew that face. Every paper in London had printed it. Jamie Harding.

A Harding.

The ball began in an hour, and the Vykra didn't give second tests. Alice turned and ran.

* * *

Harding House blazed.

A thousand green chandeliers hung over the ballroom like frozen fountains, and the light that fell from them turned the dancers into creatures from under the sea, all pale shoulders and gleaming silk.

Lord Hector Lambrick arrived in a cloud of cigar smoke. The coal baron was fifty-five and built like one of his own barges, a great barrel of a man in black broadcloth with a red face, mutton-chop whiskers and small clever eyes that priced every room he walked into. Coal was losing to Harding glass, and he knew it, and he smiled at Eldgard across the ballroom like a man who meant to change that.

On his arm was his daughter.

Tamara Lambrick was nineteen, and the most self-possessed person in the room. She was tall and slender, with black hair coiled high and threaded with jet beads, a long pale neck and a face that gave away nothing, and she wore grey silk when every other girl had chosen white. In her gloved hand was a painted fan, and folded inside the fan, where only she could see it, was a brass cipher wheel. She broke codes for pleasure the way other girls embroidered.

She and Nathaniel had been engaged by their fathers. They met in the middle of the floor and looked at each other with perfect understanding.

"I propose," Tamara said, "that we be very polite to each other for forty years."

"I accept," said Nathaniel, "with relief."

At the door, Captain Nixon Hargrave stood on duty in his new collar and watched Tamara Lambrick. When she looked back, he looked away.

* * *

In the service corridor behind the ballroom, a lamp-girl in a borrowed apron was lighting wicks.

Alice had got in through the kitchens with a tray of spills and a story. Nobody looked at lamp-girls. That was the point of them.

Then there was a crack and a shower of sparks, and the young man she was paid to get close to came backing out of an alcove with his arm on fire. The gauntlet on his forearm spat blue light into the chandelier overhead. Crystals rang. A matron screamed.

Alice did not think. She caught his wrist, popped the housing with her thumbnail, and bent a hairpin across the shorted contacts. Under her breath, so low only he could have heard it, she counted.

"One post, two post, three, four."

The sparks died. Four seconds.

He stared at her. "Who taught you that?"

"Nobody teaches lamp-girls anything, sir."

Something passed between them, quick and hot as the spark, and a lie went under it. He didn't ask about the counting. He kept it.

* * *

From the gallery above the ballroom, Josephine Harding watched the room.

She was fifty, and she was beautiful the way a well-made knife is beautiful. Her hair was dark gold going to silver, swept up and pinned with a single emerald comb. Her gown was black velvet cut close to the throat. Her gloves were black kid, buttoned to the elbow, and she never took them off in company. She stood very straight and very still, and her eyes moved over the crowd below the way a hawk's move over a field.

Mostly, they moved over the lamp-girl.

"That girl," Josephine said, "holds her wrist like a knife-girl."

Byron, beside her with a silver tray, did not look down. "You'd know, madam."

One look passed between them. Thirty years were in it, and an agreement never to mention them.

Below, the engagement was announced. Glasses rose. And Nathaniel stepped forward into the light with the gauntlet on his arm.

"Father. If I may. I've built something I'd like you to see."

Eldgard smiled for the room. "Not tonight. Toys after supper."

Laughter rippled out across the ballroom. Kind laughter, which was worse.

Nathaniel's face did something small and terrible, and then it was fixed again. On his way out of the light he passed Nixon at the door. Nixon had seen all of it.

Nathaniel smiled at his oldest friend, and the smile cost him. "He pinned your lantern himself today. You're the son he actually wanted, Nix."

Nixon laughed it off. It was the last time either of them would find that funny.

Across the room, Jamie Harding slipped out of a side door. Only Nathaniel saw him go.

* * *

Deep below the Harding Works, Jamie went down.

He had a key cut from an old Guard key, and he used it on gate after gate, down iron stairs that grew colder with every turn, until his breath smoked and the rail burned his palm. At the bottom, the dark was full of moving green light, and full of a sound.

A woman, weeping.

He did not look at what was weeping. He went to the lock, a great iron lock where the chain was sealed, and he drew a blade of clear crystal from his coat and began to cut. Skrrk. Skrrk. Skrrk. The blade bit, and bit, and stuck fast in the iron.

Jamie looked at it. Then he opened his own palm on its edge and pressed his bleeding hand flat against the lock.

The chain shuddered.

* * *

In the ballroom, every chandelier guttered at once.

A gasp went round the room. Then the green went out, and all of London went with it, street by street, a wave of darkness rolling outward from Tyburn to the river. Trams died. Windows went black. Somewhere a horse screamed.

In the black ballroom, Nathaniel Harding heard a woman crying inside the walls.

Nobody else did. He turned in the dark, looking for his father, and his father was already gone.

* * *

Two hundred guests were shouting for lamps.

Then, fssst. One match flared. The lamp-girl, cupping it in her palm, the only light in the room.

Nathaniel found her by it. "Do you hear that?"

She listened, honestly. She heard rich people frightened of the dark.

"Hear what, sir?"

"Someone crying."

"In this house? Half the room is, sir. They've lost their light."

He nearly laughed, and that surprised him more than the dark. "Who are you?"

"The girl who lights your wicks."

"That isn't a name."

"It's the only one your house pays for."

"Then tell me one for free."

She looked at him over the flame as if she were pricing him. The match burned all the way down to her fingers. She didn't flinch. He noticed that, too.

"Ask me again when the lights come back on."

She blew it out. Fff.

In the black, her voice was suddenly close to his ear. "Congratulations on your engagement, sir."

Her hand was in his coat, and then it wasn't. When the next match flared across the room, she was gone, and so was the folded schematic of his leap-gauntlet. He wouldn't miss it until morning.

* * *

At the Works, the alarms were howling. Saboteur in the core.

Nixon led his squad down the gantries at a run, rifles up, Cole at his shoulder with his heart in his throat. The dark was total. Then the flash-bursts started.

Fzzt. Fzzt.

Each one froze the dark into a picture. Pipes. Steam. A gantry rail. A masked figure at the lock, far below.

"Step away!"

Fzzt.

The figure turned, and something glinted in his hand.

Nixon fired his lantern rifle.

KRAK.

The figure dropped.

* * *

On Blackwood Bridge, at midnight, Miriam Bounds waited in her red wool mittens.

Jamie didn't come.

The lamps were dark along the rail. The river slid under the bridge, black and silent. And frost began to creep up the iron, a white fur spreading along the rail, then up the hem of her coat, then up her sleeves. Her breath came out in clouds. Her teeth began to chatter.

She heard it then. Weeping. Low and patient, from everywhere and nowhere.

A small girl made of frost was standing beside her.

Miriam looked down at her. She bent close, shaking, and asked her something, too low for anyone else to hear.

The girl nodded.

* * *

In the core, Nixon knelt and pulled off the saboteur's mask.

It was Jamie Harding. Alive, and bleeding.

Nixon's whole body went cold. He could not make his hands move.

Eldgard Harding stepped out of the dark behind him. "Go, Captain. Wait at the gate. Speak to no one."

Nixon went.

On the gantry above, forgotten, Cole Pruett pressed himself against the rail and watched. He saw the man on the floor lift his head. He saw him say something to his father.

Eldgard knelt beside his son.

Cole looked away. He would wish, later, that he hadn't.

* * *

Alice ran the whole way from Mayfair in her lamp-girl's apron, the stolen schematic still in her bodice, the city black around her.

She found Miriam on Blackwood Bridge.

Her sister was frozen solid where she stood, eyes open, frost in her lashes, standing in the middle of a perfect ring of white on a night with no frost anywhere else in London.

Alice screamed. It went on for a long time, and nobody came.

* * *

At the Works gate, Eldgard came up out of the dark and stood beside his captain.

"Jamie fell," he said. "The saboteur escaped. You never saw his face." He laid a hand on Nixon's shoulder. "You'll be the man who kept this family's secret, Captain. And this family never forgets."

Nixon nodded.

The leash went on.

* * *

At grey dawn, in the drawing room of Harding House, Eldgard told his wife and his son. An accident. In the core. Jamie had gone down alone, and fallen.

Josephine looked at her husband one beat too long.

Nathaniel heard the weeping again, and this time it was in the floor, under his feet, under the carpet, under the house. He walked out of the room very steadily. He made it as far as the stables before he broke.

Nixon found him there, on his knees in the straw, and held him while he shook. His best friend's brother's blood was still under his fingernails.

From that morning on, every scene those two shared had a bomb under the table.

* * *

In the city morgue, Inspector Felix Hawke pulled back the sheet.

He was forty, a lean grey heron of a man in a rumpled overcoat with the collar turned up, his moustache drooping, his eyes red-rimmed from a night without sleep. He had seen a great many dead in his time, and he looked at this one gently, the way he looked at all of them.

"Is this your sister, miss?"

Alice nodded. She reached out and touched Miriam's hand.

The frost melted under her fingers. Drip. Drip.

The dead girl's eyes moved, and found her.

"Ali."
