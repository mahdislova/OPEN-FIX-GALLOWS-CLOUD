Episode Eight. The Wedding.

Fourteen days to first snow. The seventh of December.

* * *

Sixteen years earlier. The core.

A boy of five in a white nightshirt came down the forbidden stairs in his bare feet.

He was small for five, with a mop of dark curls and his mother's watchful eyes, and he was following a song. He had heard it in his bed, coming up through the floor, so soft and so sad and so beautiful that he had climbed out from under his blankets and padded down the back stair, past the kitchens, past the coal-hatch, down and down through the iron gates that someone had left unlocked, to see who was singing.

At the bottom, he found a huge, gentle woman made of ice.

She looked down at him from her hooks with eyes of green fire. He wasn't frightened. Children often aren't, of the right things.

"What is your name, little one?"

"Nathaniel."

She sang it back to him. Nathaniel. Long and low, like a lullaby, like a lamp being lit.

Then a hand closed on his collar and he was snatched up into the air. His father. White-faced, breathing hard. Eldgard carried him up the stairs, into the dark of the gallery, and sat him on a bench. He took a lantern from the wall and swung it, slowly, back and forth, before the boy's eyes. Back and forth. Back and forth.

"You had a dream," Eldgard said softly. "You never heard anything."

The boy's eyes followed the light. His face went smooth and glassy.

"I had a dream. I never heard anything."

It was the nightmare Nathaniel had had all his life. And it was the reason that, of everyone in his house, he was the one who heard her cry.

* * *

The night before the wedding, in the glasshouse, nobody said a word about it all evening.

He was marrying Tamara Lambrick in the morning. The stove ticked. The orange trees stood in their tubs. Alice cleaned her gauntlet. Nathaniel mended a valve. Nobody said anything.

Then the creature, who had never seen a wedding, decided she wanted to practise one.

She found a prayer book on a shelf, left by Nathaniel's grandmother forty years ago, and she held it upside down, and she stood on an upturned flowerpot to be the priest. She made Alice stand in for the bride.

"Dearly beloved," the creature read, with enormous solemnity, from the upside-down page. "We are gathered here."

Alice rolled her eyes. But she stood where she was put.

Nathaniel stood opposite her.

And they said every word to each other. Slowly. All of it. And both of them meant every word, and both of them knew it.

"Wilt thou have this woman to thy wedded wife?"

"I will."

"Wilt thou have this man?"

"I will."

The creature turned the page, very pleased with herself. "You may now kiss the—"

"That part isn't ours," said Alice.

They stood there. Not stopping. Not moving, either.

The creature looked from one to the other, sincerely puzzled.

"You both look sad when you look at each other. Is that what weddings are for?"

Nobody answered.

In the morning, his mother would have a piece of paper for him.

* * *

In the groom's dressing room at Harding House, Josephine straightened her son's tie.

He was in a black morning coat with a white rose in the buttonhole. She was in dove grey, gloved, perfect. She tied the knot, and pulled it tight, and smoothed it flat with her fingers.

Then she gave him a piece of paper.

It was Grise's receipt, written out in a clerk's neat hand. For a contract. On her own son.

The second Harding son. Agent: Alice Bounds.

Alice.

It was the first time he had ever seen her first name. And it was on a bill.

"She was paid to get close to you," Josephine said. "By me. Whatever she's felt, I bought it."

Nathaniel couldn't breathe.

His mother wanted his eyes on the altar today. And the lamp-girl's eyes on him. And nobody's eyes on the glasshouse. Grise already knew the hour.

Josephine left. And Eldgard took her place in the doorway, holding something in his palm. Jamie's cufflinks. Gold, with the Harding crest.

Nathaniel looked at them. "Did you hold his hand?"

Eldgard said: "I held his hand."

It was the half-truth he lived in.

Nathaniel took the cufflinks and put them on, and smiled, because he knew what today was for.

* * *

He went straight to the glasshouse with the receipt crushed in his fist.

Alice was at the door with her coat half on.

"Were you paid?"

She looked at the paper. She looked at him.

"At first."

She started to say the rest of it. He was already gone.

He hadn't called her Bounds. He hadn't called her anything.

Alice stood in the doorway with her coat half on.

Behind her, the creature had heard all of it. She had also heard Alice say the word cage, in this very room, a week ago, and she had thought about nothing else since. Now she made it easy.

"Go," she said. "Watch him. If you don't, you'll hate yourself. And then you'll hate me." She smiled. "I'll be here."

"Mittens on," said Alice. "Door locked."

"Yes, Ali."

The third lie.

Alice went.

* * *

At dawn on the wedding day, in his room at the Guard tower, Nixon Hargrave finally opened the folder.

He had carried it against his chest for a week. He untied the string and laid it open on his knee.

His charity-school blood test. A sketch of a green sliver of glass. And beneath it, in Eldgard Harding's own hand:

Hargrave, N. Cadet line of Harding. Reserve. Blood suitable.

He read it three times.

He had never been a charity case. He had been a spare.

Then a knock, and a Crown messenger, and orders from the Lord Chancellor, arriving while the folder was still open on his knee. Arrest Eldgard Harding after the vows. Then clear the crowd at the gates. By any means.

Nixon folded the file away inside his coat.

"I'm done," he said, "being anybody's reserve, Cole."

* * *

In the vestry of the Harding chapel, before the guests arrived, Tamara Lambrick came looking for somewhere to be sick.

She was in white, a long column of ivory silk, a veil of old lace pinned in her black hair. She was very pale.

She opened the vestry door and found Nixon there. Uninvited. In his captain's coat. Standing over a little velvet ring box on the vestry table, waiting for a best man nobody had named.

She shut the door behind her.

"Say one word," she said, "and I'll walk out of this church with you."

The folder was in his coat. He could feel its corners against his ribs.

"This morning I found out I'm on Eldgard's books," he said. "Like the coal. I can't give you something that belongs to somebody else."

She looked at him for a long moment.

Then she picked up the ring box. Opened it. Closed it. Put it in his hand and closed his fingers around it.

"Then hand it to me properly."

She went out to be married.

At the chapel door, Josephine was waiting to settle her veil. She peeled off one glove to do it, to have her fingers free for the lace.

Stitched inside the glove was a Vykra knot.

Tamara had carried a needle with that knot's poison on it since the gallery stairs at the hearing. Her mother-in-law's bare fingers settled the lace around her face, gently, carefully.

Both women knew. Neither said a word.

Behind them, the vestry stood empty. Within the hour, it would hold a strait-coat.

* * *

The Harding chapel was full of lilies and candles and the Crown.

Two hundred guests in silk and broadcloth filled the pews. Lord Chancellor Vane sat in the front pew in black, with his pince-nez on its ribbon. Eldgard sat alone, unaware.

Nathaniel stood at the altar in his morning coat with Jamie's cufflinks at his wrists.

Nixon walked in, uninvited, up the side aisle, and stopped behind the groom he hadn't spoken to since the roof.

Nathaniel didn't turn. "Stand where I can see you."

Nixon stepped up beside him.

A whisper. "You look terrible."

"I haven't slept."

"Neither have I."

Nixon stood there and waited for the bride he had kissed a week ago.

And up in the gallery, at the very back, where a lamp-girl would stand, was Alice Bounds in a borrowed hat.

* * *

In her pew, Josephine turned, and found the lamp-girl at the gallery rail.

And she smiled at her. For the first time, ever.

Alice didn't know what it was for.

She would know in an hour.

* * *

At the gates of Harding House, a crowd had gathered from the undercity.

Some of them carried candles, for the Frost Witch who had stopped the wolves in Whitechapel. Others were coal-men with clubs, who wanted her burned.

A line of Lanterns stood between them, rifles at port.

* * *

At the groom's shoulder, Nixon saw the vestry door ease open.

Ajax Fink stepped out of it, in the room where Tamara had asked Nixon for one word an hour ago. Behind him came two Bethlem attendants in grey, carrying a strait-coat, and Ajax held a paper. A lunacy order. For Tamara Lambrick, spinster. Her father's signature as nearest relative. Two doctors'. And the Chancery seal. Because lunatics were the Lord Chancellor's wards, and the Lord Chancellor was sitting in the front pew.

Lambrick didn't need a Harding son-in-law any more. Since the gasworks he sat on the Crown's board, and the Works were half his. What he needed was a daughter who couldn't read his books aloud. And he had waited for the one room in London where the Lord Chancellor could seal her mad in front of everyone.

Nixon whistled their old signal, very soft. Two notes. Danger.

Nathaniel looked. Saw Ajax. Understood. They had the length of one question.

In the front pew, Eldgard saw it too, and half rose. A marshal's hand settled on his shoulder, and he sat back down.

* * *

Across the lawn, in the glasshouse, nobody was watching.

The creature sat under the orange trees with her mittens off, humming the Lantern Hymn to them in a key no church uses. She was waiting.

When Grise's girls came through the glass, five of them, quiet, with knives and a sack and Josephine's directions, the creature didn't fight.

She stood up. She held out her wrists.

And she breathed once on the pane by the door. Frost wrote itself across the glass.

She couldn't have walked to the core. It was Crown iron now. And the cage that could put her in her mother's place was in Crown hands. The only way in was as somebody's prisoner.

And if she had gone with Alice in the room, Alice would have followed.

So she went with them the way Miriam went to the bridge. On purpose, and without saying goodbye.

* * *

In the chapel, the priest raised his eyes to the congregation.

"If any man knows cause, or just impediment—"

Lambrick stood up.

He spoke gently, to the room, a grieving father. "My daughter is unwell."

The attendants came up the aisle with the strait-coat. The priest stopped with his mouth open, looking to Vane.

Nixon's hand was on his rifle. His company was at the gates. He could carry her out of this chapel in a minute.

Nathaniel saw him think it, and shook his head once.

Carry her out, and she was a runaway lunatic with a Chancery seal on her for the rest of her life. But the order named a spinster. Make her a wife, and it named nobody. And her nearest relative would be her husband.

It had always been the plan. It just had to happen in the next thirty seconds.

Nathaniel looked up, and found Alice at the gallery rail.

She nodded. Once. Small. Like a permission.

She was letting him go.

"Go on," he told the priest. And then, not waiting for him: "I will."

He said it the way he had said it last night among the orange trees. And at the gallery rail, Alice's lips moved with his.

Tamara, steady as a bell: "I will."

Nixon stepped into the aisle, square in the attendants' path, and was very slow and very polite about getting out of it.

"Pardon me. So sorry. If you'd just—no, after you. Pardon."

Then he turned and held out the ring. Properly.

"Congratulations, miss."

"Thank you, Captain."

The ring went on.

Vane looked at the order in Ajax's hand. Then at the bride. Then at two hundred witnesses. Voiding a marriage in front of them would mean a Chancery hearing, and a Chancery hearing would go on the record.

"This is addressed to a spinster," Vane said. "I see a wife."

The attendants folded up the strait-coat.

When Nathaniel looked up again, the gallery rail was empty.

* * *

Alice ran.

Out of the chapel, down the steps, out through the gates where the riot was starting, across the lawn. Ten minutes too late.

The glasshouse door hung open. The orange trees were dusted white, as if it had snowed indoors.

On the pane by the door, written in frost, three words.

Don't follow me.

The same words as Miriam's letter.

Alice's knees went. She sat down in the doorway.

She had been watching a wedding.

* * *

On the chapel steps, Vane's marshals arrested Eldgard Harding for the murder of his son James. On his wife's sworn word, and a coffin full of slag.

A murderer could be hanged quietly. A Keeper who might stand up in court and read out the Crown's annex could not be left free. And with Eldgard in Newgate, nobody would be left to argue with the men who now owned the core.

The marshals took his watch, his cane and his collar studs. They left the wolf-crest ring on his hand. Nobody outside the family knew what it had closed.

He looked at his wife, and he understood.

"Josie."

She didn't look away.

* * *

Nixon walked out of the chapel still holding the empty ring box.

The crowd at the gates saw Eldgard in irons and surged. Coal-men and Frost Witch believers clashed, candles against clubs. Somebody threw a stone. Somebody screamed.

Vane, on the steps: "Lanterns. Fire."

Nixon: "Lanterns. Stand down."

Vane's second company raised their rifles at the crowd. Nixon's company raised theirs at Vane's.

A long moment. Every rifle cocked. Click, click, click, click.

Then Cole Pruett walked out between the lines with his hands up. His bandaged hand, two fingers short, held high.

Nobody fired.

Vane came down the steps, white with rage, and ripped the green-glass lantern off Nixon's collar.

"Keep the flame, Captain."

Nixon looked at him. "I am."

Behind him, forty Lanterns tore their own lanterns off their collars. They dropped them in the snowless gutter, forty little green lights in the dirt. And they walked away with their captain.

* * *

In the old nursery at Harding House, the newlyweds sat alone with the wedding breakfast. A cold chicken. A cake nobody had cut. Two glasses of champagne going flat.

"We said forty years of politeness," said Tamara.

"We managed four minutes."

"Thank you."

"Don't."

She looked at her plate. "I love Nixon."

"I know. I watched him hand me the ring."

One unsaid thing, finally said.

At the door, he found her own shawl already laid over the armchair he would sleep in. Neither of them mentioned it.

Across the lawn, a lamp came on in the glasshouse.

He stood at the window and watched it, holding a candle, until the candle burned down to his fingers. He didn't flinch.

Then he went.

* * *

He found Alice on the glasshouse floor, under the frosted pane.

"What did you see up there?" he asked.

"You, being brave," she said. Before either of them had to say what it had cost.

He read the glass.

"She heard me say cage in this room," Alice said. "She's gone to get in it."

"Then it fails," said Nathaniel. "No mother lets her child hang in her place."

"She went because she wanted to. She thinks she can make her."

It was Alice's own lie, love as holding on, coming back at her from the other side of the glass.

* * *

Behind the Works, alone, in the dark, Nixon Hargrave found the coal-hatch stair.

He had been stripped of his rank an hour ago. The old man's rhyme was going round and round in his head. Thirteen steps down where the cold water drips.

He lifted the hatch. He counted down.

One. Two. Three.

The walls were wet. Somewhere water was dripping. Plink. Plink.

Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

Behind the last stone, there was a door. Behind the door, the Orchard.

A long low gallery under the earth, lit by his single lamp. Iron-barred bunks in rows, stacked three high, like shelves in a larder. Child-sized shackles on the walls. And on the far wall, scratched into the stone, hundreds and hundreds of names, and tally marks.

Each name was a Hargrave.

The first, cut deep and worn almost smooth by four hundred years: Marian. Fourteen eighty-nine.

The tallies beside the names were a keeper's count. And over them, in a keeper's chalk, one word.

APPLES.

Count the apples.

The children had been the apples.

A skipping rhyme every London child knew went through his head. Green light, green light, who's in the cellar tonight?

He was standing in the cellar.

The last name on the wall was Marian, too. With a date four years after he had been told his mother died of fever. For four hundred years, the Orchard had named its girls after the first one who tried.

He sat down on her bunk.

Creak.

Under the thin pillow, there was a wooden spoon. Small. Worn smooth.

And over the lintel of the door, in old chalk, two initials. J. H.

Jamie had been here first.

Nixon understood what he had been picked out of. And who had kept it from him.

* * *

In Newgate, Josephine visited her husband through the bars.

He sat on a plank bed in his shirtsleeves, without his collar studs. The wolf-crest ring was still on his hand.

"You made a deal with Lambrick," he said. "The girl to the chain, our son kept out of it, and me to Newgate."

"Yes."

"You sold me."

"I sold the man who sold our son."

He looked at her for a long time. Then he asked one thing. "Was any of it real?"

"Thirty years," said Josephine. "All of it."

It was the first time in a generation neither of them lied.

"Our son lives," she said.

Eldgard shook his head slowly. "You've killed him. I've stood in front of her for forty years, and she's never once given a Harding anything he could keep. She'll break that glass before she lets her child hang in it. And when it breaks, they'll need a Harding anyway."

"Four hundred years in hooks," said Josephine. "Nobody refuses the door."

Eldgard looked at her.

"You would."

It landed. Josephine's face, for one moment, showed the thing she never let it show. That she might have made a mistake. And that it was too late to unmake it.

* * *

In the core, Lambrick and Vane stood on the gallery and watched the Heartglass close.

Nathaniel's design. Ribs of crystal and brass, folding in around the creature like the petals of a great glass flower, beside her mother's hooks.

The creature looked up at Morgana. And she spoke to her in the language of the White.

"I've come to take your place."

Morgana understood. For four hundred years, she had wanted the door. And here it was. Held open by her own child.

She screamed.

It wasn't rage. It was grief.

And it was no.

* * *

The scream rose up through the Works, through the iron and the glass and the stone, up into the dark sky over London.

And it began to snow.

Two weeks early.

Alice stood in the glasshouse doorway with snow falling on her upturned face.

Nixon and his forty stood on a rooftop in Whitechapel, their faces lifted.

Tamara stood at a window in Harding House in her wedding dress, her palm flat on the cold glass.

Josephine stood on the steps of Newgate, very still, as white flakes settled on her black veil.

And in his cell, Eldgard Harding stood at the barred window with snowflakes landing on his face. He had counted to the solstice, like every Keeper before him. In four hundred years of ledgers, she had never once been early.

"First snow," he said.

Whatever was owed was owed by midnight.

Zero days.
