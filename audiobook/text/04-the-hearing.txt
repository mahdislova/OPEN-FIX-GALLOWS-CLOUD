Episode Four. The Hearing.

Thirty-eight days to first snow.

* * *

The second of November. Ten minutes before the blackout.

Fog lay thick along Tyburn Road, yellow-green in the lamplight, and the iron gates of the Harding Works loomed out of it like the gates of a prison. A black coach waited at the curb with the Lambrick crest on its door, its horses stamping and blowing.

Inside, Lord Lambrick had already paid his man. Two hundred guineas, for Ajax Fink to cut the outer grid tonight and shame the Hardings in front of their own ball.

Ajax stood by the coach wheel, counting the gold into his palm. Clink. Clink. Clink.

Then a young man stepped out of the fog. Fair hair. A good coat. Jamie Harding.

"Whatever he's paying you," Jamie said, "I'll double it."

Ajax raised an eyebrow. He kept counting.

"Cut it at one exact minute," Jamie said. "Eleven forty. The whole city dark at once. And tell him nothing."

Ajax looked at him for a long time. A whole city going dark, so that nobody would look twice at one Harding going down to the core inside it.

"His Lordship says when the light dies," Ajax said, "London comes back to coal."

Jamie smiled. "Then London can freeze honestly."

He held out a second purse. Ajax took it.

Two men had bought the same blackout for opposite reasons, and only one of them knew what was under the Works.

* * *

The Harding glasshouse stood at the far end of the estate, behind a wall of yews, forgotten.

It had been an orangery once, a long Victorian barrel of glass and white iron, built by Nathaniel's grandmother and abandoned when she died. Half its panes were cracked. Ivy had got in at one end. But the orange trees were still alive in their great tubs, dark-leaved and stubborn, and when Nathaniel lit the old iron stove, the air filled with the smell of warm earth and citrus and rust.

It was the last place his father would look.

The creature walked in under the glass and stopped. Frozen butterflies were stuck to the panes, dozens of them, wings spread, perfect as brooches. She touched one, gently, through the glass. Then she held out her palm, and frowned at it in concentration, and above it a little flurry of snow began to fall. Real snow. Tiny white flakes, turning in the air.

She laughed. Like a child at a magic-lantern show.

Alice and Nathaniel stood by the stove and watched her. And they had their first real conversation, about everything except the thing between them.

Alice gave him her theory. The one she had built to live inside.

"Something came back in her from the cold. It's got its own wants. But Mim's under it. Wren says the memories come back."

Nathaniel had felt that girl hold his bare hand without freezing it. He looked at Alice with something like pity, and didn't argue.

Later, by the stove, the creature tried again. Very carefully.

"Ali. Under the bed—"

Alice handed her an orange. "Eat that."

Behind the fence.

* * *

At breakfast in Harding House, under the green chandelier, Eldgard folded his newspaper.

"The Crown's hearing on the Charter is in three days. You'll sit beside me. As heir."

Josephine buttered her toast. "And the Lambrick wedding moves up. January."

"No," said Nathaniel.

The table went silent. The footman stopped pouring. It was the first open defiance of his life.

Josephine didn't answer him. She was reading the hearing's witness list, and her eyes had stopped at one name.

Inspector F. Hawke. On the death of James Harding.

A bullet hole, shown in front of the Crown, meant a Crown inquiry. A Crown inquiry would go down the core stair. And at the bottom of the core stair, it would find the Tithe, waiting for her last son.

She folded the list in half, and in half again, and put it in her sleeve.

* * *

In the armoury of the Guard tower, among the racks of rifles, a Crown order arrived from the Lord Chancellor.

After the market deaths, the Frost Killer was to be shot on sight.

Cole was back on duty, bandaged, two fingers short and desperate to be useful, and Nixon put him on the roster anyway, to give him back his pride. Cole saluted with his good hand, and this time he didn't hit himself in the eye.

Then Tamara Lambrick came into the armoury, in a dark blue walking dress and a hat with a veil, and Nixon's whole body went still.

She told him. Ajax Fink had been in her bedroom. Her father had paid Ajax on the night Jamie Harding died.

She stood close. Closer than she needed to. The only man in London she trusted.

Nixon wanted to say three things, and said none of them.

"I'll put a man on your door, miss."

"I'd rather you were the man."

Silence. The rifles ticked in their racks as the room warmed.

* * *

Back at the glasshouse workbench, Alice laid out her Vykra intelligence. Ajax Fink collected his money from the coal docks on Thursday nights. Alone.

Nathaniel nodded. He didn't say anything. He went to his bench, and for two days he built her a gauntlet. A real one. Not a coughing bootleg ghostlight, but a proper Lantern gauntlet, brass and steel, with a gallowglass core that hummed like a bee in a jar.

He fitted it to her hand, strap by strap. Wrist. Forearm. Elbow. His fingers on the buckles.

"Hold still, Bounds."

She did. She had never held still for anyone in her life.

It was a love scene with no kissing in it.

* * *

Late that night, Alice fell asleep at the workbench with the new gauntlet still strapped to her arm, her cheek on a pile of schematics.

The creature sat with Nathaniel by the stove. The glasshouse ticked and creaked around them as the iron cooled. And out of nowhere, in Miriam's voice, she gave him something of his brother's.

"He talked about you. On the bridge stair, when he was cold. He said you'd fly before you ever learned to walk. And that you were the only one of them worth saving."

Nathaniel had spent his whole life believing he was the spare.

"He said that?"

"To me."

She was careful not to say who me was. He didn't think to ask.

He sat with it until the stove went out. It was the first time anyone had ever told him he was worth something without wanting something back. And it had come out of a dead woman's mouth.

* * *

At the Shadwell laundry, Mother Grise did not offer tea.

"Bring me the frost girl."

"No," said Alice.

Grise looked at her for a long, long moment. Then she reached out, took Alice's left hand, turned back the cuff of her glove, and with one snip of her sewing scissors, she cut the Vykra knot out of the lining.

Snip.

"Then you're no one's."

Alice walked out into the rain with nowhere to belong.

Under the dripping awning of a pawnshop, she stopped. She took out the handkerchief, his handkerchief, white linen with grey initials, and she folded it small, very carefully, and tucked it into her glove. Into the place where the knot used to be.

* * *

Thursday night. The coal docks at Wapping.

Black barges lay three deep along the wharf, heaped with coal, and the cranes stood over them like gallows. The river slid past, oily and slow. Fog came and went.

Ajax Fink strolled down the wharf with his hands in his pockets, whistling.

Nathaniel hit him from the left. Alice hit him from the right.

The Black Gauntlet swung.

WHUNK.

It hit like a steam hammer. Nathaniel flew six feet backward into a coal heap. He came up spitting black, and stared at the gauntlet on Ajax's arm. He knew those pistons. He knew those rivets. He had drawn every one of them himself, two years ago, and then his prototype had been stolen out of his shop.

"That's mine," he said.

"Finders keepers," said Ajax, and swung again.

WHUNK. WHUNK.

Nathaniel was losing. Badly.

Then Alice raised her new gauntlet, the one he had strapped onto her arm two nights before, and fired a flash-burst straight into Ajax's eyes.

FZZZT.

He reeled, blind. Together they got a crane hook through his belt, kicked the brake, and swung him out over the black river, upside down, dangling.

* * *

Ajax hung there, swaying, and laughed.

"Your brother paid me," he said. "Lambrick paid me too, but your brother paid double. I cut the outer grid. I never touched him." He spat into the river. "Your father went down after. Ask the Guard who fired."

Alice grabbed Nathaniel's arm. "Pull him up. He's the only witness we have."

Nathaniel's hand was on the brake lever. Two true things were pulling at it. The man who helped kill his brother. And the one voice in London that could put Lambrick in the dock.

"He's Jamie's grave," Nathaniel said.

He let go.

The brake slipped. The cable ran. Ajax dropped into the Thames.

Splash.

The black water closed over him. The Thames kept him, for now.

* * *

In his father's study, Nathaniel stood in front of the desk with coal dust still on his collar.

"Jamie paid Lambrick's man. To cut the grid. Why?"

Eldgard didn't look up from his papers. "Your brother was ill. Melancholy. He wanted to destroy this house."

It was half true. That was how Eldgard lied.

Then he looked up, and offered the bait Nathaniel had wanted all his life.

"After the hearing, I'll take you into the work. All of it."

On the back stairs afterwards, Josephine stopped Byron with a hand on his sleeve. "Where does Nathaniel go at night?"

"To his workshop, madam. He works very late."

It was the first time in thirty years Byron had ever lied to her.

* * *

Newgate Prison. The debtors' wing. Cell fourteen-seventeen.

Nixon went alone, out of uniform, in a plain brown coat, with the visitor's slip in his pocket. The turnkey led him down a long stone corridor that smelled of piss and tallow and despair, to a barred door at the very end, where the light didn't reach.

The prisoner was an old man. Seventy, if a day. Tobias Marsh was a bundle of bones in a filthy blanket, bald and spotted, with a wild white beard down to his chest and eyes as bright and mad as a bird's. He sat cross-legged on the stone, plucking at a single piece of string stretched between his fingers. He called it his harp.

Plink. Plink.

"Jamie Harding came here every Sunday," Nixon said. "Why?"

The old man hummed. He looked up at Nixon. Then he scuttled forward with startling speed, reached through the bars and pinched Nixon's cheek, hard, between finger and thumb.

He cackled.

"Marian's chin! Marian's chin!"

Nixon went very still. "What did you say?"

The old man sang, in a high cracked voice, rocking:

"Seven in the Orchard, ripe for the fall.
One on the tree for the lantern's call.
Thirteen steps down where the cold water drips.
Count the apples, kiss the stones' lips."

Nixon grabbed the bars. "Enough. Why was Jamie Harding visiting you? Tell me, you old fool."

Marsh only cocked his head and smiled. "He cried too, you know. Same place in the song."

Then he turned his back, and went back to his harp. Plink. Plink.

Nixon left with a nursery rhyme, and a cold feeling he couldn't name.

* * *

Felix Hawke tracked Alice to the glasshouse.

He came through the yews at dusk in his overcoat, his sling, his patient grey face. Alice met him at the door with her gauntlet raised. He held up his good hand.

The creature came out from among the orange trees. She looked at his bandaged hand, and she remembered it. The door. The frost.

She held out an orange.

"I'm sorry about your hand," she said.

Felix looked at her. A frightened girl, holding out fruit.

He was already listed to testify about the bullet hole in Jamie Harding's coat. Now he would say more. "Someone made her," he said to Alice, quietly, "and I'll say so in front of the Crown."

And with that, Inspector Felix Hawke became the most dangerous man in London.

Outside, in the dark under the garden wall, Josephine Harding had heard every word.

She had known for a week where her son went at night, whatever Byron told her. She said nothing to her husband. A Frost Killer dragged out of the Harding glasshouse would take her son to the dock beside her. So the girl would have to be caught somewhere else.

* * *

The hearing was held in a committee room in the Palace of Westminster, all dark panelling and green leather and tall windows streaming with rain.

Lambrick stood and argued that the Hardings were hiding something under the Works. Something unnatural. Something the Crown ought to see.

Eldgard was calm. Eldgard was always calm.

Lord Chancellor Vane presided, and never once asked what.

Nathaniel sat beside his father, the heir. Tamara watched from the public gallery. Nixon's Lanterns stood at the doors in their green coats. And in the back row of the public seats sat a veiled woman in black.

At the recess, Felix Hawke took his place in the witness box, grey and tired, his notes in his good hand. The veiled woman crossed the room with a glass of water.

"You look pale, Inspector."

He smiled at her, grateful. He drank.

* * *

The hearing resumed. Felix stood.

"My lords. The deaths at the market were caused by a girl who should be dead. And the Harding Works—"

He coughed.

He coughed again, and put his hand to his mouth, and when he took it away, his lips were white. Frost was spreading across them, delicate as lace. His eyes went white. He swayed, and gripped the rail, and frost bloomed on his fingers where they touched the wood.

He died on the stand. In front of the Crown.

Somebody screamed. "The Frost Killer! The Frost Killer is here!"

Doors slammed. Lords scrambled over benches. The gallery surged for the stairs. Nixon's Lanterns fired flash-bursts into the ceiling to stop the stampede, FZZT, FZZT, FZZT, and the whole room strobed white.

Vane, on his feet, white-faced, ordered the Frost Killer hunted "with every lamp in London." The vote was postponed. And by evening, all of London's sympathy had swung toward the poor, persecuted Hardings.

In the crush on the gallery stairs, Tamara was pressed against a veiled woman in black, and she saw the woman's glove. And inside the glove, where the cuff turned back, stitched in fine black thread, a Vykra knot.

Something dropped onto the stair at their feet. Tink. A needle.

Tamara picked it up, and put it in her pocket.

* * *

Nathaniel ran all the way to the glasshouse with the news. Felix Hawke was dead. Killed by frost. On the stand.

Alice turned and looked at her sister. The creature had been here with her, among the orange trees, all day long.

Alice knew the poison. She knew it by the description alone. She had been taught it in the laundry.

"Winter's kiss," she said. "That's Vykra."

So somebody was framing her sister.

* * *

On the roof of the Guard tower, in the rain, Nathaniel found Nixon.

"Ajax told me something. On the crane. He said: ask the Guard who fired."

Nixon went very still.

"Find me the name, Nix."

"I'll find it."

That night, in the armoury, Nixon found it. The rifles had come back from Nathaniel's shop that week, refitted, with their discharge logs bound into the cases, every round accounted for. He opened his own rifle's log.

One round. The second of November.

He tore out the page. He opened the stove door and fed it to the flames, and watched it curl and blacken and go.

From the bunk-room doorway, in his nightshirt, Cole Pruett watched him do it.

Later, by a stub of candle, Cole copied the page from memory into his diary. Every word.

* * *

At the Shadwell laundry, Tamara Lambrick walked in out of the rain and laid a needle on Mother Grise's counting table.

"One of yours killed a policeman in front of the Crown," she said. "I want to know who. And I want in."

Grise looked at her for a long time. At the jet beads in her hair. At the fan in her hand, with the cipher wheel inside it. At her eyes.

Then she smiled.

Alice had been cut out of the Vykra yesterday. And Tamara Lambrick was the new girl.

* * *

In her dressing room at Harding House, Josephine sat down before her mirror and lifted her veil.

She peeled off her black kid gloves, one finger at a time. Inside the left glove, stitched in fine black thread, was a Vykra knot.

She dipped her fingertips into the porcelain bowl on her washstand and rubbed them together, gently, carefully, washing away a fine white dust.

The water in the bowl skinned over with ice. Krrrk.

In the mirror, behind her, Byron was standing in the doorway.
