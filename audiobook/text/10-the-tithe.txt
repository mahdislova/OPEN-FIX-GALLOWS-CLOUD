Episode Ten. The Tithe.

The last night.

* * *

The core. Fourteen eighty-nine. An hour before the lantern was first lit.

Alistair Harding stood at the chain with his net still wet with frost. In the net, curled up small and shaking, was a child made of ice.

Morgana hung fresh in her hooks above him. The iron was new. The wounds were new. Her blood had only just begun to run.

She looked down at him, and she made him an offer.

"Free me, and pay in light. Keep me, and pay in sons."

Then she said the part no Harding ever wrote down.

"Either way, my child goes free tonight."

Alistair looked at her for a long time. Then he slid the wolf-crest ring onto his finger, and turned it once, so the wolf's head faced out.

"Sons."

He cut the net.

The frost child slipped out of it and was gone, up the stair, into the dark, toward the white beyond the gallows.

In the doorway behind him, his firstborn son stood waiting. Edric. Nine years old. The boy with the lantern. He held it up for his father, solemn and proud. And then he turned and carried it down the stair ahead of him. Alone.

It was the same choice Nathaniel would face, four hundred years later, tonight.

* * *

The core. Ten minutes to midnight.

The lever dropped.

The Heartglass flooded the creature with green fire, and she screamed. And the door swung open for her mother. The hooks loosened, in Morgana's shoulders, in her hips. They slid. For the first time in four hundred years, Morgana could step out.

She didn't.

She took the chain in both her great hands and dragged herself back onto the iron. The hooks tore deeper. Green blood poured. She pulled against the trade with everything she had, until the glass around her daughter cracked, from her side.

Krrrrrk.

"Not her."

The chain shuddered, and didn't take.

At the lever, Josephine watched a mother refuse the door. And she heard her husband's voice, in a cell in Newgate. You would.

From the gallery, Lambrick shouted down. "Then the boy! He'll walk if it saves somebody. Take the girl he came for."

Vane's marshals started down the stairs. For Alice.

* * *

Alice smashed the Heartglass with her gauntlet.

KSSHHH.

Crystal exploded in a glittering cloud. She reached in through it and dragged the creature out, and the creature came out half turned to crystal, her arms glassy, her face cracked like porcelain, her eyes still Miriam's.

Josephine drew her knives.

The marshals were already on the stairs to make her son walk. A broken cage was still a cage, until midnight. She came at Alice to put the girl back in it.

And they fought. Vykra against Vykra. The mother against the girl she had paid to love her son. Knife on gauntlet, blade on brass, in the green light of the pit. Josephine fought like a woman who had just been shown she was wrong.

Josephine was better.

Then Nathaniel stepped between them.

"Mother."

* * *

Nixon came down the stair bleeding from the scalp, Tamara behind him.

He walked out into the middle of the core floor and stood there in front of all of them. In front of the marshals, and Lambrick, and Vane, and Morgana in her hooks.

"I'm Harding blood," he said. "Take me. They grew me for this, the way they grew my mother. Let me be something on purpose."

He knew he was doing exactly what Eldgard had grown him to do. He did it with his eyes open.

Behind him, on the stair, Cole Pruett said: "Sir. No."

Lambrick, from the gallery: "Harding blood is Harding blood. Let him."

The marshals turned toward the captain.

* * *

"No one goes in the chain again," said Nathaniel.

He went to the lock.

Jamie's glass blade was still in it, where his brother had left it on the first night. Frozen into the iron by the cold that had leaked out of it for four hundred years.

Nathaniel took hold of it and pulled. It didn't move.

Krrk.

His hands slipped on it.

Alice was beside him before he asked. And the gauntlet he had built for her in the glasshouse, strap by strap, was already whining up to overload. Eeeeeeeeeee. The way he had told her never, ever to run it.

He knew what she was about to do.

"If this works," he said, "every light in London goes out. They'll hate me for a hundred years."

"Us," she said.

She put the gauntlet's core flat against the iron.

The heat came back through the plate into her palm, and she held it there. The leather of her glove smoked. The smell of it, burning. Her face went white. She held it there, until the ice around the blade let go.

Crack.

He pulled the blade free. He cut his own palm open on it. He pressed his blood and his father's wolf ring into the iron.

He knew what his father had told him it cost. When the chain breaks, one of the house goes with her.

And his father's last warning was in his ear. Hoping.

He couldn't swear that his brother wasn't part of why his hand was steady.

Harding blood had closed it. Harding blood opened it. But it had taken an undercity girl's burned hand to get the blade out.

Somewhere far above them, a church bell began to strike twelve.

* * *

The chain fell.

And London went dark.

The last time London had gone dark, the light had come back within the hour. This time the green died street by street, the same wave as the night of the engagement ball, rolling outward from Tyburn to the river. And nothing came back.

A tram died in the snow on the Strand, its windows going black one by one.

A hospital ward went dark in the middle of an operation.

On Gin Lane, a mother lit a tallow candle with shaking hands, and the little yellow flame was the only light on the street.

Under the Works, Nathaniel Harding listened.

For the first time since he was five years old, nothing was crying in the walls.

* * *

Morgana rose out of the pit.

The hooks fell away from her one by one, clang, clang, clang, and her wounds closed over as she rose, ice flowing back into ice. Nine feet tall. Then more. She stood up out of four hundred years, and her head brushed the vaulted ceiling of the core.

She looked down at Nathaniel. And she said his name, the way she had sung it to a five-year-old on the stair.

"Nathaniel."

Then: "A Harding opened it."

She didn't thank him.

The White poured through behind her. Wind. Snow. Wolves. A howling gale of white that filled the core in a heartbeat. And she went up, through the roof of the Works, smashing iron and glass, up into the storm, toward Tyburn hill.

Where the chain had been anchored, the core floor split open, and the White came up through the crack in a wall of wind.

Alice and the half-crystal girl were on one side of it. On the outfall side. Nathaniel and his mother were on the other.

Alice couldn't see across it.

The girl in her arms was going colder by the minute. The outfall was the only way open. And the only fire Alice could count on tonight was on Gin Lane, where the Vykra were holding.

She took it.

* * *

On the core stair, Nixon met Ajax.

Quench-blade against Black Gauntlet. Glass against iron.

Ajax swung. WHUNK. Nixon ducked. The gauntlet took a lump out of the wall.

Ajax was laughing. He was always laughing.

Nixon went for the quick takedown, the one every Lantern learns as a Wick. He drove his quench-blade into the gauntlet's crystal core. The piston froze solid. And then he drove the blade into Ajax.

Ajax Fink died frozen in the middle of a laugh.

His apple dropped from his other hand and rolled down the stairs, bump, bump, bump, all the way to the bottom.

With Ajax dead, the Guard stair was clear. The one way out of the Works the wolves hadn't found.

Nixon put two fingers in his mouth and whistled down it. The third signal. The one they had made up at nine years old, and never once needed.

Come on.

* * *

London, the night the snow came early.

Nixon's forty, and every loyal Lantern in the city, formed flash-lines across the bridges to keep the wolves off the tenements. A line of men on Westminster Bridge. Another on Blackfriars. Another on Southwark. FZZT. FZZT. FZZT. All night long, the river flickered.

The Vykra held Gin Lane with knives and candles.

Alice held the lane with them. Behind her, in a doorway, the creature lay wrapped in coats, as close to the coal fire as Alice could get her.

Between wolves, Alice grabbed a girl by the arm. The tea-girl from Covent Garden. Mouse-brown hair, a burn scar on her wrist, and a knot in her glove.

"Go to the Works. Find out if he's alive." A breath. "Don't tell him I asked."

The girl went.

Alice was three steps after her before she knew she was moving.

Behind her, a child began to scream. Aaaaah.

She turned back to her knife.

Every time a flash lit up the sky over Tyburn, she looked.

All across the city, Tamara Lambrick threw open every Lambrick coal depot, free. There were fires in every street. On Gin Lane, the Vykra held the wolves at a ring of Lambrick coal, burning in the snow. Without it, the lane would have frozen before the wolves ever reached it.

And in the middle of it all, in a doorway full of frightened children, Cole Pruett held up his flash-gauntlet set to low and taught them to make shadow rabbits on the wall. Two ears. A nose. A hop.

The children laughed.

* * *

The charges ran out along Cole's line.

He was holding Tyburn Road with a barricade of overturned trams, the last thing between Gin Lane's fires and the hill. He held it with his last cartridge, two fingers short and seventeen years old. Until it was empty.

Behind his line, a girl in a Vykra glove came up the road through the snow, carrying another girl in her arms. Half crystal. Heading for Tyburn. An hour before dawn, by the fire, the creature had woken, and asked for her mother.

Cole knew her. The ghostlight girl from the market. The one who had fought beside him at the Works gate.

He didn't know why she was carrying the Frost Witch toward the hill. He didn't ask.

He held the road until she was past it.

Nixon found him in the last dark before dawn, sitting up against an overturned tram, as if he were resting. His diary lay open in his lap.

Under the copied log page, there was a last line in pencil, in a careful seventeen-year-old's hand.

Held the road. Nobody past who shouldn't be.

* * *

In the core, empty now, Nathaniel and Josephine stood alone.

The hooks hung empty from the walls. Snow fell through the broken roof.

"I'd do it all again," said Josephine.

"I know," said Nathaniel. "That's why I can't be you."

At the foot of the stair, she stopped. "I paid for that girl."

"You paid for a week, Mother. You couldn't afford the rest."

Josephine almost smiled. It was the first thing about her son she had ever failed to buy.

Boots on the stair. The tea-girl, bleeding from the scalp, out of breath.

"Gin Lane's holding. Just." She hesitated. "She said not to tell you she asked."

Nathaniel was at the stair before she had finished.

And Josephine, who had controlled every door in his life, said: "Go to her."

It was so unlike her that he stopped, with his hand on the rail, and turned and looked at her. Really looked. And he saw a woman who had already decided something.

"Not until you tell me where you're going."

She wouldn't.

So he stayed. He didn't go to the girl he loved. He watched his mother, the way she had taught him to watch everyone.

He was still watching when she started up the hill, by the Guard stair Nixon had cleared.

* * *

On Tyburn hill, Morgana stood over London in the storm.

The old gallows field. The place where it all began. Snow whirled around her. A ring of white wolves circled her, slowly, their eyes blue in the dark. Below, the city lay black from end to end, without a single green light in it. Only fires. Little orange fires, of coal and tallow, scattered across London like embers in a dead grate.

Four hundred years of grievance stood on that hill.

Josephine climbed toward her. Nathaniel climbed after his mother.

And up Tyburn Road, past the barricade a seventeen-year-old had held to his last cartridge, Alice came up the hill carrying the Lost One.

Across the snow, they saw each other. For the first time since the lock. Alive.

There was no time for any of it.

* * *

The Lost One set herself down in the snow, in front of Alice. Between her mother and the city.

The place Miriam never once stood.

Alice saw it. Her breath went.

The Lost One lifted her face to her mother, and she sang. The hymn. The Lantern Hymn. But not with London's words. She sang it back in the old tongue, the way it had been before it had words. A mother's lullaby, sung by a daughter.

The wolves stopped circling.

"They let you go," the Lost One said. "Let them go."

"They bled me for four hundred years," said Morgana.

"One of them let you go."

* * *

In the vault of the Works, in the last hour before dawn, Lord Lambrick was filling satchels.

He had been at it all night. Bags of gallowglass, the last there would ever be. Cut off from the chain, it was dying, slowly. Every crystal in the vault was a shade dimmer than it had been at midnight. And he was packing them as fast as he could, as if he could outrun the dawn.

Tamara found him there.

She stood in the doorway in her father's greatcoat, over a ruined wedding dress, and held out her hand.

"Come out, Father."

He held up a satchel to her. Almost pleading. In his hands, the glow was going out while they talked.

"This is for you. All of it. It was always for you."

"Then leave it."

He looked at the crystals. He looked at her.

He couldn't put them down. He never could.

She walked out.

Behind her, the wolves came into the vault.

* * *

On the hill, a mother was being asked the same question.

"Then come home, little frost," said Morgana.

The Lost One looked at Alice.

And Alice, who had spent the whole season holding on, let go.

"Go, if you want to," she said. "I'll be all right."

She meant it. She had done this once before. With a nod, at an altar.

* * *

The Lost One told her the last thing.

"I've spent everything else of hers. The birthdays. The songs. Her name for you. I kept this."

Snow fell between them.

"On the bridge, she was still alive. She asked me something. Go back to Ali. Stand in front of her. I never did." A breath. "I said yes. Then I wanted to."

Miriam's last thought had been Alice, after all. And her last act had been to find someone braver, to keep the promise she broke.

Alice laughed and cried at once. Her sister did love her. She just couldn't do it herself.

The Lost One stayed.

And Morgana, who had torn her own hooks rather than let her daughter pay, let her stay. Which was harder. And she didn't leave her the cold to stay with.

She knelt in the snow, and laid one great hand flat on her daughter's chest, and drew the White out of her, the way you'd draw a splinter.

The crystal went out of the girl's skin. Her arms went soft and pink. Her face lost its cracks.

She would never make snow again. She would never again hear her mother singing in a lamp.

What was left was a girl. Shivering, for the first time in her life.

Then Morgana stood, and turned, and named the price.

"A Harding chained me. A Harding comes with me."

She waited. It had to be given. She would not take it.

Nathaniel stepped forward.

And Alice stepped in front of him. Into the place nobody had ever stood for her. Her fist in his coat.

Her first word to him all night.

"Don't."

* * *

Josephine stepped past them both.

She walked up to Morgana through the snow and the wolves, and she said to her the words Morgana had said at the lever.

"Not him."

Morgana looked down at her for a long moment. And she knew her.

Josephine turned to her son.

"I'm a Harding," she said. "I chose the name."

He caught his mother's wrist.

Alice was still in front of him, her fist still in his coat. Two women holding him, one from each side of his life. And both of them were right.

The wolves shifted their weight in the snow.

Josephine spoke quietly. It was the only time all season she asked him for anything.

"They'll say I died of fever in Switzerland."

"No," said Nathaniel. "They'll say your name."

A long beat.

His hand opened.

"Go."

She kissed his forehead.

Then she turned to Alice. "Keep him out of the Works."

The contract she had paid for, all those weeks ago. Now given as a blessing.

Alice said: "You bought me for him. It's the only thing you ever bought that worked."

Josephine almost laughed. And that was how she went.

She took Morgana's hand. And they walked together into the White.

The wolves followed. And the storm went with them.

* * *

On Tyburn hill, dawn came.

The snow stopped in the middle of falling. It just stopped, and hung, and drifted down the last few feet, and lay still.

Nathaniel was on his knees where his mother had been.

For a long time, nothing happened.

Alice didn't touch him. She knelt in front of him in the snow and let him have it, because nobody had ever let her.

When it came, it wasn't crying. It was a sound with no word in it. Hhh. Hhh. Hhh. A man trying to breathe around something too big for his chest.

She stayed where he could see her, until it passed.

Then she took the handkerchief out of her glove. His handkerchief. The one he had given her at his brother's grave.

She tore it in two. Rrrrip.

She bound half of it round the palm he had cut at the lock.

He took the other half out of her fingers without asking, and bound the hand she had burned for him.

Neither of them was any good at it.

She didn't know what to say to a man who had just said go to his mother. So she gave him the most private thing she owned.

Under her breath, the way Miriam had taught her under the bed, she counted for him.

"One post. Two post."

He knew it. He had heard it in a ballroom corridor five weeks ago, and never asked.

He counted with her.

"Three post. Four post. Five. Six. Seven. Eight."

At eight, she stopped. Because eight was where it had always ended.

He kept going.

"Nine."

Nobody had told him about nine. He had got there on his own.

And then, for the first time, he said her name.

"Alice."

She heard it. He had never once said it. And she knew which bill he had read it on.

He was saying it now. When nobody had paid for it.

He kissed her. Or she kissed him. For the rest of their lives, they would disagree about which.

* * *

At Westminster, at first light, grey dawn came through the tall windows of the Lord Chancellor's office. Coal smoke from Tamara's fires was rising over the city.

Lord Chancellor Vane named Nathaniel Harding "the man who put out London," and signed a warrant.

Then Tamara, Lady Harding and heir to Lambrick, laid the Heartglass folio on his desk, with the Crown's seal on the binding order.

Beside it, she laid her father's ledger, open at the night of the Charter vote. A.F. Pimlico. Three hundred guineas. The man the Crown had put on the Works board had blown in its windows, and his daughter said so, with his name and hers.

Last, she laid down a needle. Vykra issue. The one she had picked up on the gallery stairs the day Felix Hawke died.

Nixon stepped forward and laid Cole's diary beside it, taken from the dead boy's lap. The burned discharge-log page, copied word for word. And under it, in a seventeen-year-old's careful hand:

Second of November. Captain hit him in the shoulder. Mister Harding sent the Captain to the gate. The man on the floor was still talking.

It put Nixon's shot on the record. And the man who had stayed behind with a living boy.

Nixon could have burned that page a second time. He set it down open.

Across the desk, Nathaniel looked at him. The first time he had looked straight at him since the lock.

"I haven't forgiven you."

"I know."

"Stand where I can see you."

Nixon stepped up beside him, the way he had at the altar. And it meant the opposite now.

Vane slid a captain's lantern across the desk toward Nixon. Green glass, polished. The price of his silence.

Nixon left it where it lay. "The Guard will keep its own flame."

Vane, cornered, turned to Nathaniel and offered the pardon. On one condition. The Crown would seal what had happened under Tyburn. For the public good.

"No," said Nathaniel. "Write it down."

"The Crown doesn't keep that kind of record."

"It has kept one since sixteen eighty-nine. It's in your desk. Under your signature."

Vane didn't blink. An annex in a drawer had never frightened a Lord Chancellor.

Then Tamara, who had not sat down, spoke.

"Lambrick depots are feeding every grate in London this morning, my lord. They close at noon. Unless that record is read into Hansard. All of it. Where anyone with a penny can buy it."

Outside the tall windows, the coal smoke was the only warmth in the city, and every chimney of it was hers.

Vane looked at the smoke for a long time.

"The House sits at two."

Nathaniel turned to the clerk. The clerk dipped his pen.

"Edric Harding," said Nathaniel.

* * *

This is the record. Nathaniel's voice, dictating. At first, flat and exact, like a ledger. And somewhere in the middle of it, it stops being for the clerk.

"Edric Harding. Nine years old. The year of our Lord fourteen hundred and eighty-nine. He carried the lantern down the stair himself, because his father asked him to."

At the Works, snow fell through the broken roof onto empty iron hooks. The channels where her blood had run into London lay dry and grey.

"The Hargraves of the Orchard. Their names are scratched on a wall under Tyburn. I will read every one of them, if the clerk has ink."

In the Orchard, old Tobias Marsh held up a candle and read the names off the wall, one by one, perfectly lucid now. Nixon wrote them into Cole's diary. His thumb stopped on one. Marian.

"Ottoline Harding. Nineteen. Eighteen thirty-nine. She did not die abroad."

In St Bride's, a mason set his chisel to a brass plate, and tapped. Tink. Tink. Tink. Died abroad came off it one letter at a time. Byron's blue ribbon was tied through the bracket.

"James Harding. My brother. He tried to end it so I would never have to, and we said he was ill. We buried a coffin of slag and called it him. There was no body. I don't know where he is."

In the long gallery of Harding House, Jamie's portrait laughed at something outside the frame.

"Inspector Felix Hawke, of Scotland Yard. He stood up in front of the Crown to say that someone had made the Frost Killer, and he was poisoned on the stand for it. My mother did it, for me. London blamed a frightened girl."

At Scotland Yard, Felix's sling still hung on the back of his chair. A constable took the Frost Killer bills down off the wall, one by one.

"Josephine Harding. My mother. She walked into the White on the night the snow came early, so the debt would end. She will have no brass that lies."

In the music room, the piano lid was closed. On it lay a Vykra knife, and a pair of black kid gloves with the knot stitched inside.

"For four hundred years this city was warm because somebody was cold. We called it light. We wrote a hymn over her crying, so we'd never have to hear it."

On Gin Lane, the mother from the night before lit a second tallow candle off her first, and passed it through a broken pane to her neighbour. And the neighbour lit another, and passed it on. Window to window, the flame travelled down the lane. Under it, the Gin Lane children skipped rope in the snow to the old rhyme, with a new last line.

Green light, green light, nobody's in the cellar tonight.

"Every one of us lived on it. Most of us never knew the price. Some of us knew, and kept the lamps lit anyway."

In the Works vault, a satchel lay half buried in a snowdrift, with a man's glove still on the strap. The crystals spilling out of it had gone as dull as river stones.

"I can't give you the light back. I won't. What I can give you is the bill, and the names of the people who paid it."

At a Lambrick depot, the gates were thrown open. Tamara, in her father's greatcoat over a ruined wedding dress, signed the ledger as heir while the coal went out to the queue for nothing. Alice stood beside her at the gate. Tamara held out her hand, wrist up, the way the laundry closes a contract. His name on her paper, until the coal ran out. Alice took it. Tamara looked up from the book, and she was the one deciding now.

"So write them down. Keep them where your children can read them. And the next time somebody offers you a light that never goes out, ask who is paying for it."

On the roof of the Guard tower stood forty Lanterns with no lanterns. Nixon hung Cole's battered Wick lamp on the rail, and struck a match, fssst, and lit it. A plain candle flame. Nothing else. Tamara stepped out onto the roof. She didn't take his hand. She stood beside him, close enough that it didn't matter.

"We will be cold for a while."

At St Thomas's Hospital, a surgeon worked by six candles that a nurse held close. His hands were shaking with the cold. He breathed on them. Hhhh. And went on.

"We'll build something that doesn't need a girl in chains, or a boy on the stair. It may be worse. It will be ours."

In the Shadwell laundry, Alice sat stitching a new knot inside her own glove. A pattern nobody had taught her. Mother Grise, without being asked, poured her a cup of tea.

"That is the record. Let it stand."

In Whitehall, the record was read into Hansard that afternoon, and by evening it was on every newsstand in London. When his door had closed behind the last of them, Lord Chancellor Vane took the sixteen eighty-nine annex out of his desk. He read one line of it. He locked it back in the drawer, and put the key in his waistcoat pocket.

The record stood. So did the Crown.

* * *

At Harding House, cold and dark, Nathaniel dropped the wolf-crest ring into the dead grate. Clink.

Alice set Jamie's ring, the one from Miriam's cord, on the mantel beside it.

His bound hand came to rest over her bound hand. Two halves of one handkerchief.

There was no inch between them now.

* * *

On Gin Lane, in the morning, the Lost One sneezed.

Achoo.

She looked astonished. She had never sneezed before. She was warm, for the first time in her life.

Then she was sick in a basin, exactly the way Miriam had been, on the morning of the second of November.

Alice laughed. And she peeled her an orange, badly, in one long ruined spiral, and handed it over.

Then she stopped. And she put her hand on her sister's belly.

Under her palm, very faint, something was beating.

Thup-thup. Thup-thup. Thup-thup.

The cold that had held Miriam frozen had gone out of her. And what it had been holding had started again.

Jamie's child.

The Lost One looked down at Alice's hand. "Is that me?"

"No," said Alice. "That's someone else."

* * *

The White.

Wind, and nothing else. Endless snow, under a sky with no sun in it.

Josephine walked beside Morgana through the drifts, to the place where the chain's dead went, and stopped.

A man was standing in the snow with frost in his hair.

Jamie Harding. Exactly as he went down. Twenty-five, fair-haired, in his shirtsleeves, one hand still half raised, the way it had been on the lock.

His breath didn't smoke. His eyes were open, and didn't move.

Josephine touched his face. It was cold, all the way through.

Morgana, beside her: "The White keeps what it's given. It doesn't give it back."

Josephine didn't let go of him.

Far behind him, small in the white, a boy of nine stood with a lantern raised. The flame was still burning after four hundred years. And the boy was as still as the snow.

Josephine sat down in the drift beside her son, the way she had once sat on the bank of the Serpentine holding his scarf, and she did not take her eyes off him.

* * *

Day one.
