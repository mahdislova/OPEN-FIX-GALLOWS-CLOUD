Episode Three. Lanterns.

Forty-two days to first snow.

* * *

Ten years earlier. A charity school in Southwark.

The hall was long and bare and smelled of carbolic and boiled oats. Thirty boys stood in a line along the wall in grey smocks and bare feet, oldest to youngest, the cold coming up through the floorboards into their soles.

A gentleman in a black coat walked slowly down the line. Eldgard Harding, younger then, his beard still dark. At each boy he stopped, took the small hand in his own gloved one, and pricked the fingertip with a silver needle. A bead of blood. He touched it to a sliver of pale gallowglass held between finger and thumb.

The sliver stayed dark. He moved on.

Dark. Dark. Dark.

Then a thirteen-year-old boy with sandy hair and a split lip, standing very straight because somebody had told him gentlemen liked boys who stood straight. Eldgard pricked his finger. Touched the blood to the glass.

The sliver glowed. Faintly, but it glowed. Green.

Eldgard smiled and patted the boy's head. "Still good."

The boy's name was Nixon. He would remember that smile for the rest of his life as the first kindness anyone had ever shown him. It was something else.

* * *

In the gauntlet shop of the Harding Works, Eldgard laid a sheaf of orders on his son's bench.

"Re-equip the Guard," he said. "Leap-rigs. Flash-gauntlets. And refit their lantern rifles. All of it. For the Frost Killer hunt."

Nathaniel looked at the papers. Then at his father. His first real job. His whole face lit up like a lamp.

Captain Hargrave was waiting at the door for his own orders. Eldgard walked him out into the corridor, out of his son's hearing, and gave them quietly.

"Find the frost girl. Bring her to the Works. Alive. Tell no one. Not the Yard. Not my son."

"Yes, sir." Nixon hesitated. And then, because it was the only thing he had to give, he gave it. "I've seen her, sir. At our own gate, five nights ago. She put her hand on it, and every lamp on Tyburn Road leaned toward her."

Eldgard went very quiet.

"Then she'll come back to it."

Nixon owed, and now he was owned. So the real son was arming the hunt, the spare was leading it, and neither of them knew that the girl Alice Bounds was hiding was its target.

* * *

Alice found her sister on a rooftop pigeon loft, deep in serious conversation with the pigeons.

The creature sat cross-legged among the droppings and the feathers in Miriam's brown coat and Miriam's red mittens, cooing earnestly at a fat grey bird, which cooed back. She seemed to think they were getting somewhere.

They wrote the house rules together in chalk on the loft wall. Mittens on. No touching. Cocoa allowed. The creature added a fourth in careful capitals: PIGEONS ARE FRIENDS. Alice let it stand.

Then the creature held something out.

An orange. Peeled, badly, in one long ruined spiral, the peel hanging off it in a ragged ribbon, the flesh gouged where her mittened thumbs had dug too deep.

"It was your birthday," she said. "On the bridge day. I forgot."

Alice stared at it.

A piece of Miriam's regret, surfacing in a creature that didn't know why her hands had wanted to do this.

"You've never once remembered," Alice said.

"I know."

Alice took the orange and ate the whole thing, pith and all, and it was the best thing she had ever tasted.

The creature watched her, head tilted. "The light is crying, Ali. Can't you hear it?"

Alice listened. All she could hear was pigeons.

* * *

Lambrick House stood on the north side of Belgrave Square, heated by coal, every room of it, out of principle.

At two in the morning, Tamara Lambrick knelt in front of her father's cipher safe in her nightgown, a candle held in her teeth, her ear against the steel. Her fingers turned the dials. Tick. Tick. Tick-tick. She did not breathe.

Ninety seconds. Click.

She didn't copy just the line. She copied the whole ledger page. Six months of payments to A.F. Every one of them. The last of them on the second of November, the night Jamie Harding died.

On her way back up to bed, on the dark stairs, she found a man sitting on the landing, eating an apple.

He was thirty-five, long and loose-jointed, with a lazy ruined handsomeness to him. Black hair worn too long, a three-day beard, a Guardsman's greatcoat with the badges ripped off, and on his right arm, from knuckles to elbow, a gauntlet of black iron, all pistons and rivets, big as a coal scuttle. His eyes were amused, patient and quite empty, like a cat's.

Ajax Fink. Her father's man.

"Evening, miss," he said, and crunched his apple.

He let her pass.

* * *

On the roof of the Guard tower, the Wicks were learning to fly.

Nixon had six of them lined up in Nathaniel's new leap-boosters, brass and leather rigs strapped to their backs like beetles' wing-cases. Nixon stood at the end of the line with his hands behind his back.

"On my mark. Not before. On my mark. Three. Two."

Kshhh, THOOM.

Cole Pruett fired early.

He sailed off the roof of the tower in a beautiful wobbling arc, arms windmilling, and came down across the alley into a cat's cradle of laundry lines strung between the tenements. Sheets tore. Pegs popped like gunshots, pok-pok-pok. And there he hung, upside down, wrapped in a stranger's petticoat, with a pair of enormous flannel bloomers draped over his head.

Below, a woman leaned out of a window and screamed bloody murder at him.

Nixon laughed. A real laugh, the first since the core, loud and helpless and from the belly.

Then he remembered. And he stopped.

* * *

In the Shadwell laundry, Mother Grise poured tea and did not offer Alice any.

"Your report on the Harding son."

"He knows nothing," Alice said. "He builds toys. His father doesn't let him near anything."

Grise sipped. "Three days. Bring me something real." A pause, as gentle as a knife sliding into a sheath. "Or the frost girl on Gin Lane becomes my business."

So Alice would have to use Nathaniel.

* * *

She went to the Works the next afternoon with his handkerchief in her pocket, to return it.

He was so pleased to see her it was almost painful. He showed her everything. He showed her the new leap-rigs, the flash-gauntlets, the refitted rifles in their racks. And then, because she was watching, because nobody ever watched, he strapped on a booster and jumped.

Kshhh, THOOM.

Across the foundry, over the benches, in a long beautiful arc. And straight into the coal bin again.

Whump.

He came up black from head to toe, grinning. An audience of one. Nobody had ever wanted so badly to be watched by her.

While he was brushing off soot, she read the sweep roster on his bench, upside down. Gin Lane. Tonight.

She felt sick at how easy it had been. She felt sicker at how much she had liked watching.

She left without returning the handkerchief.

* * *

At Scotland Yard, Felix Hawke cornered Nixon in a corridor, his frostbitten hand in its sling.

"The Frost Killer is Miriam Bounds," Felix said. "She was dead, Captain. I had her on my slab."

Nixon thought he was mad. He said so, politely.

Then Felix lowered his voice. "Something else. The Harding boy didn't die in a fall. There was a bullet hole in his coat, Captain. Somebody burned the coat. But I saw it first."

He took a slip of paper from his pocket and slid it across the window ledge. A Newgate visitor's slip. Jamie Harding. Every Sunday, for two years. Prisoner number fourteen-seventeen.

"Someone ought to ask why," Felix said, and walked away.

Nixon stood in the corridor with the slip in his hand. Both his secrets were walking toward each other, and he could hear their footsteps.

* * *

On the Serpentine in Hyde Park, the ice was thick and grey, and the fashionable world went round and round on it in furs.

Tamara had taken Nathaniel skating, because engaged couples went skating, and they were doing what was expected.

He was terrible at it. Truly, magnificently terrible. His ankles bent like wet straw. He clutched her arm. He fell on his backside, whump, and slid six feet on it, and lay there looking at the sky.

Tamara looked down at him without a flicker. "You can fly across a foundry."

"Flying is easy. It's the landing."

She helped him up, and while she did, she slid a folded paper into his coat pocket.

The ledger copy. Six months of payments. A.F.

"Ajax Fink," she said. "My father's man. Paid on the night your brother died."

So Nathaniel had a suspect now. It was the wrong one.

* * *

At dusk, the Christmas crystal market opened along the river, and the whole Embankment glittered.

A hundred stalls, roofed with canvas, hung with crystal lanterns of every shape. Stars, angels, little glass houses, birds with green fire in their bellies. Chestnut-sellers roasted over braziers. A barrel organ ground out carols. The air smelled of sugar and smoke and frost.

Alice moved her sister through it, the safest way out of Gin Lane before the sweep, head down, hat low, one hand firm on the creature's arm through two layers of wool.

The creature saw the lanterns.

A hundred crystal lights, glittering in rows, and something in her went soft and enchanted, the way it had for the cat. She reached out one red-mittened hand.

Every crystal in the market flared at once.

A great white pulse of light, like a gasp. Every face in the crowd turned.

On the roof of a warehouse above the river, a Lantern lookout lifted his whistle to his lips.

* * *

Nixon's leapers launched.

Kshhh, THOOM. Kshhh, THOOM. Kshhh, THOOM.

Green-coated men sailed over the stalls in long arcs, landing on canvas roofs, firing flash-bursts into the crowd. Fzzt. Fzzt. The market froze into snapshots. A woman with her mouth open. A spilled basket. A child on a man's shoulders.

Alice ran the roofs on her ghostlight, the bootleg gauntlet she had built from parts stolen out of Nathaniel's shop, and it coughed and sputtered under her, putt-putt-BANG, every jump a gamble.

Below her, trapped among the stalls, the creature screamed.

That glass-splitting scream.

Every crystal in the market shattered.

All of them, at once. A hundred lanterns burst into a hundred thousand pieces and came down on the crowd like hail. A chestnut-seller froze in the middle of a shout, his arm raised, his mouth open, frost spreading over his face like lace.

Four people died in the crystal market that night.

At first light, Felix Hawke walked through the broken glass with his hands in his pockets, and counted them. One. Two. Three. Four.

* * *

In the bell tower of St Magnus, above the market, Cole Pruett cornered the Frost Killer alone.

She was crouched against the great bell, shaking, her mittens torn, and she looked up at him with Miriam's eyes, and she reached for him.

"Please."

He took her hand. He didn't mean to. It was what you did when someone said please.

Cole froze from the hand up. White crawled up his wrist, his arm, his shoulder. His lips went blue.

Nixon landed on the parapet, kshhh, THOOM, and fired a flash. The creature dropped, stunned, against the bell.

And Nixon stood between them. On one side, the Frost Killer, out cold, everything his patron wanted, lying at his feet. On the other, a seventeen-year-old boy turning blue, his heart slowing.

He could take the girl to Eldgard. Or he could carry Cole down to the furnace room before the boy's heart stopped.

He took Cole.

Alice came over the parapet a moment later, grabbed her sister under the arms, and dragged her away into the dark.

* * *

The next morning, the papers at Harding House screamed it in letters an inch high.

FOUR DEAD AT CRYSTAL MARKET. FROST KILLER STRIKES AGAIN.

In the servants' hall, the maids were saying the Frost Killer had been helped by a dark-haired girl with a ghostlight rig.

Nathaniel sat at the breakfast table with the paper in his hands and remembered a hairpin. A gauntlet fixed in four seconds. A voice in the dark, counting.

Byron poured his tea. "You're your mother's son, sir. You notice."

* * *

Alice hid her sister in the one place nobody would look: right under the Hardings.

There was an old lamp-tunnel beneath the Works, bricked off and forgotten, damp and black and full of the hum of the mains overhead. She wrapped the creature in coats and held her, gloves on, and the creature lay against her shoulder and shook.

Four people were dead in the market.

Alice held her and said the thing you say. "You didn't mean it."

It was the wrong sentence.

It was what Miriam used to whisper under the bed, about him. And it unlocked something in the body.

Something ran down the creature's face, and froze.

Tink.

A bead of ice, falling onto the stone.

Tink. Another.

She caught one in her mitten and stared at it. "What is this?"

Alice, very quietly: "You're crying, Mim. You always cried. Under the bed, you cried for both of us."

And the creature was suddenly full of a memory she had never lived. A buckle. A small girl's back, very thin, the knobs of the spine showing. And her own body, not moving. Not moving at all.

"Ali. The belt. I should have—"

Alice went hard as iron. "We don't talk about him. That's behind the fence."

The creature didn't know what the fence was. She knew only that she had just been shut out of something that hurt, and that she wanted in, to make it stop hurting.

She remembered a woman in chains, crying, too. She said nothing about that one.

Alice held her until the ice stopped falling.

* * *

At Guy's Hospital, Cole Pruett woke in a narrow white bed with his left hand wrapped like a pudding.

He had lost two fingers. The little one and the ring finger.

"Did I do good, sir?"

Nixon sat beside the bed in his muddy coat. "You did good."

Eldgard Harding was standing in the doorway.

Out in the corridor, under a green lamp, Eldgard said, "You had her, and you let her go."

Nixon said nothing.

Then Eldgard leaned close, and very softly, the way you'd speak to a child at bedtime: "I know what you did at the core, Captain. So do you."

* * *

At the mouth of the old lamp-tunnel, Nathaniel Harding was waiting.

He had found it the way an engineer would. Her ghostlight ran on his design. It whined at his pitch. He had spent the whole night walking the lamp-tunnels under his own family's Works with a brass listening horn pressed to his ear, until, very faintly, he heard it.

It had been built from the schematic that left his coat in the dark at his engagement ball.

Alice came out of the dark with her knife up. Then she saw who it was, and lowered it, a little.

She told him the truth as she believed it. Her sister had nearly frozen to death on Blackwood Bridge and come back wrong. She was hiding her until she got better.

Then the creature stepped out of the tunnel behind her. And before Alice could stop her, she reached out, and took Nathaniel's bare hand.

Alice's heart stopped.

Nothing happened.

No frost. No white crawling up his wrist. His hand stayed warm in hers. All three of them stared at it.

He could take the Frost Killer to his father tonight. Four people dead in the market. His father's hunt. The first real job he had ever been given, and the one thing that would finally, finally make him the useful son.

He looked at the girl holding his hand. He looked at Alice.

He didn't.

It was the first time in his life he acted against his own lie. And it was a crime.

* * *

At Lambrick House, Tamara opened her bedroom door and stopped.

Ajax Fink was sitting on her bed, his black gauntlet resting on her pillow, his boots on her counterpane.

"Your father sends his love," he said pleasantly. "Stay out of his safe."

He stood, stretched, and walked past her out of the door. He left the apple core on her pillow, brown and wet.

* * *

At the tunnel mouth, Nathaniel turned to go.

The creature lifted her head. She was sniffing the air, the way an animal does, her nostrils flaring, her eyes narrowing.

"You smell," she said slowly, "like the man who keeps my mother."

Nathaniel stopped dead with his back to her, one foot already on the stair.
