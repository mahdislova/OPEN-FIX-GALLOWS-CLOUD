Episode Five. Lantern Night.

Thirty-three days to first snow.

* * *

Tyburn. The same winter. Fourteen eighty-nine.

Torches came over the hill in the dark, a long river of them, and the frost on the gallows field turned orange in their light.

They were young, most of them. Cousins of the Harding blood, from the cadet branch the family called the Hargraves. Farm boys and clerks' sons and girls with their hair tied back, carrying pitchforks and cleavers and their fathers' old swords. They had heard the singing under the ground. They had come to stop it.

And leading them, running ahead of them all with a smith's hammer in both hands, was the girl from the treeline. Nineteen. Dark hair flying. Her face, at last, in the torchlight: fierce and plain and lit up with something like joy.

She reached the pit. She reached the chain. She swung the hammer at the lock.

CLANG.

Alistair Harding came out of the dark behind her with his sword.

Somebody in the crowd screamed her name.

"Marian!"

She fell at the lock with the hammer still in her hands.

By dawn, the empty gallows field was full. Every one of those hundred frames had its burden, and a hundred more had been built in the night, and more after that. Three thousand Hargraves hung on Harding timber, turning in the frost. Their wives and their children were walked down thirteen steps into the dark, and the door was shut behind them.

The heist we are about to watch had been tried before. It failed.

* * *

Four hundred years later, Eldgard Harding led his son into the Upper Gallery of the Works.

It was a cathedral. There was no other word for it. A vaulted hall of iron and glass three storeys high, and down its length, in two great rows, stood vats of gallowglass the size of houses, glowing from within, green and white and gold. Engineers in leather aprons walked the catwalks between them with clipboards. The air hummed. The light was so clean and so beautiful it made your teeth ache.

"This," said Eldgard, "is where we grow it. From Tyburn iron."

Nathaniel stood in the middle of it with his mouth open. His joy was real, and it was painful to watch.

"At the solstice," his father said, laying a hand on his shoulder, "I'll show you the rest."

* * *

"There's nothing down there but vats," Nathaniel said.

He was in the glasshouse, pacing, still lit up from it. Alice sat on the workbench with her arms crossed. The creature sat under an orange tree, peeling nothing, very still.

"He showed you a nice room," the creature said. "My mother isn't in a nice room."

"My father wouldn't—"

"He's lying to you," said Alice.

"He's my father, Bounds."

"She's my sister, Harding."

They stared at each other across the stove. It was their first fight, and it was about family.

* * *

In the silver pantry of Harding House, among the shelves of tureens and candelabra and wrapped cutlery, Byron took something out of his waistcoat pocket and held it out on his palm.

An old iron key, on a black ribbon.

"My Guard key, sir. From when I watched the core. Your brother borrowed it in October. He had it copied, and gave it back." He took out a second thing, a folded slip of paper, soft with handling. "With this."

Nathaniel unfolded it. His brother's handwriting.

If I don't come up, give it to Nat.

Nathaniel couldn't speak.

"I guarded the core thirty years ago," Byron said. "I heard her every night, sir. I stopped hearing it when I stopped letting myself."

He picked up a tallow candle in a pewter stick, lit it, and went back down the servants' stair. He hadn't carried a green lamp since the core. Nobody in the house had ever asked him why.

He did not mention what he had seen in Josephine's dressing room. Not yet.

* * *

In the Shadwell laundry, Mother Grise laid a sheet of paper in front of her new girl. A Harding Works lock cipher, intercepted, forty lines of numbers.

Tamara solved it in four minutes. Grise checked her pocket watch, and for the first time in a long time, she looked impressed.

"Shame they change the inner codes on Lantern Night," Grise said, idly, to nobody.

Tamara filed that away.

They welcomed her with tea, and a knife. The knife was slim and black and lay across the saucer like a spoon. For the first time in her life, Tamara Lambrick belonged somewhere because of her mind.

* * *

At the Guard tower, Eldgard gave Nixon the Lantern Night security for the Works. And then a private order.

"My son will come tonight. Let him through. Seal the core behind him. The girl doesn't leave."

"And Nathaniel, sir?"

"He's my son, Captain. He'll be with me."

* * *

That evening, the glasshouse turned into a planning room.

They built a model of the Harding Works on the workbench out of teacups and oranges. The cups were the vats. A sugar bowl was the gate tower. An orange, in the middle of it all, was the core.

The creature reached over and ate the core.

"That's the core," said Nathaniel.

"It was very good," said the creature.

Then the door opened, and Tamara Lambrick came in out of the night with the code, invited by Nathaniel.

Alice and Tamara looked at each other.

The fiancée and the undercity girl. The new Vykra, and the cut one. Every line they exchanged was polite, and every line was a knife.

"Miss Bounds."

"Miss Lambrick. Lovely hat."

"Thank you. I'm told it's the fashion to have one."

Nathaniel was the only one in the room who didn't notice.

* * *

Lantern Night came down on London like a carnival.

Every year, in the black middle of November, the city celebrated its light. Fireworks went up green over the river. Crowds packed the Strand shoulder to shoulder. The Lord Mayor rode in a gilded coach with green glass lanterns hanging from every corner, and children wore crowns of paper stars, and the whole city blazed so bright you could read a newspaper at midnight.

In the crowd near Charing Cross stood Lord Lambrick in a fur-collared coat. And beside him, very much alive, was Ajax Fink.

He had a new scar now, a pink rope of it running from his brow to his jaw. The river police had cut him out of a barge's anchor chain, gauntlet and all, and Lambrick had paid their fee. He was eating an apple.

Behind them stood a coal-mob of fifty men with cudgels, waiting.

Their plan: cut the outer grid during the fireworks finale, and let all of London watch Harding light fail before the vote.

* * *

The Tyburn conduit opened into the Thames under a stone arch at Pimlico, an old Works drain big enough for a man to walk upright. It was full of black water, waist deep and freezing, moving slowly toward the river.

The creature took off one mitten and laid her bare hand on its surface.

Krrrrrk.

The water froze. A road of black glass, straight as a ruler, stretching away into the dark under the city.

They walked in. Inside the river. Alice, Nathaniel, Tamara and the creature, their boots ringing on the ice, their breath smoking.

At the first grate, Nathaniel took out his brother's key on its black ribbon. It turned.

* * *

High on the gantries above the Works, Nixon Hargrave walked his rounds in the dark.

He saw them pass below. Four shapes, moving fast along a catwalk.

His orders were to let them through.

He put two fingers in his mouth and whistled. Two notes, low then high, the whistle he and Nathaniel had invented when they were nine years old, on a roof very like this one. It meant: turn back.

Far below, Nathaniel heard it. He looked up and smiled, because it was Nix. And he kept going.

* * *

On the Strand, the fireworks reached their finale. Rockets screamed up in green and gold and burst over the river. Boom. Boom. BOOM.

Ajax's crew cut the outer grid.

The East End went dark in the middle of the fireworks, a great black bite out of the glowing city.

Inside the Works, every lock reset at once. Clack, clack, clack, all down the corridors. And the four of them were trapped between two doors, with the Guard alarm ringing over their heads.

* * *

The inner door stood at the top of a crystal shaft four hundred feet deep.

Its lock was a wall of brass tumblers, and the code had changed tonight. Tamara knelt at the edge of the shaft and pressed her ear to the brass. To reach the dial, she had to lean out over nothing.

Alice grabbed her by the belt and held on.

Tamara listened. Tick. Tick. Tick-tick. She turned the dial by ear, listening for the tumblers falling, while four hundred feet of green light glowed below her and the alarm rang and boots thundered somewhere above.

Neither of them said thank you.

Click.

It opened.

* * *

They went down.

The stair spiralled into the earth, colder and colder, the rail burning their palms, their breath freezing on their collars. Green light came up from below, pulsing, slow, like a heartbeat.

And at the bottom, they saw her whole for the first time.

Morgana.

She was nine feet tall, and made of ice. Not white ice, but the deep blue ice at the heart of a glacier, clear as glass and dark as the sea, and inside her, where a woman's veins would be, ran green fire. Her face was long and fine and terribly beautiful, like a queen on an old coin. Her hair hung down past her waist in long ropes of frost. Great iron hooks were driven through her shoulders and through her hips, chained to the walls of the pit, and from every wound her blood ran green along stone channels cut in the floor, out through pipes, up into the city. Into every lamp in London.

She was singing.

It was the weeping. And it was the Lantern Hymn, the tune every child in London knew from church. Without any of the words.

* * *

"Little frost," Morgana said. Her voice was the wind over a frozen lake. "You came."

The creature ran to her. She grabbed the chain with both hands.

And for one second, her whole face changed. Every song Miriam had ever known came flooding back into her at once. Every birthday, every name, every summer. She was full. She was whole.

Then the chain burned her. Miriam's red mittens smoked, and blackened, and burned away to threads.

Alice pulled her back.

Morgana turned her great head, slowly, and looked at Alice.

"You have my daughter's hand," she said. "Give it back."

Alice held on tighter. "She's my sister. Whatever came back in her, my sister's under it."

Morgana tilted her head, and looked at Alice with something terribly like pity.

She didn't correct her.

The creature didn't either.

Then Morgana turned to Nathaniel, and her voice was almost gentle.

"You came down these stairs once, little Harding. And your brother is not dead. He is cold."

* * *

Nathaniel went to the lock.

It was a great block of black iron where the chain was sealed, and stuck deep in it, just where his brother had left it, was a blade of clear crystal.

He touched the chain.

It tightened. Like a living thing.

Morgana screamed. And all across London, the lamps surged brighter.

His blood was feeding it. And for one frame, in the surge, he saw something. Snow. Endless snow. And a man standing in it, very still.

Then it was gone.

He would tell himself it was the dream.

He stared at the blade in the lock, and at his brother's diagram in his memory, and suddenly it all made sense.

* * *

"Now you know what your brother knew."

Eldgard Harding stepped out of the dark beside the pit. He had been waiting.

He told his son the Tithe. Every fifty winters, at first snow, a Harding walks down to the chain of their own will. Or it breaks.

"And if it breaks, the light dies for good. The White comes through until dawn. And it takes one of us with it."

He looked at his son. "I raised Jamie for it. Jamie ran. Now it's you."

Nathaniel stared at him. "Why not you?"

Eldgard didn't answer.

The silence went on a beat too long. Nathaniel filed it away.

* * *

The core door sealed with a hiss. Iron on glass.

Behind the glass stood Nixon Hargrave, following orders. His lips were moving. Light the dark. Keep the flame. Light the dark. Keep the flame. Over and over, loud enough in his own head to drown out whatever Nathaniel was shouting.

"Nix!"

Nathaniel's fists hit the glass.

Nixon didn't look away. And that was worse.

* * *

The creature let go.

Not of Alice. Of the cold. All of it. Everything she had been holding inside Miriam's body since the bridge came pouring out of her at once, a white roar.

The whole core froze around her mother. Frost raced up the walls, across the floor, over the chain, over the hooks, over Morgana herself.

Morgana screamed, the way she had screamed when Nathaniel touched the chain, only worse. So much worse. Her blood flared through every channel at once.

And across London, on Lantern Night, every lamp in the city blazed white for one single heartbeat, and burst.

Pop, pop, pop, pop, pop.

Street after street. A wave of shattering glass rolling outward under the fireworks. Tram lamps. Shop lamps. Church lamps. The Lord Mayor's lanterns. The chandeliers of Mayfair and the bare bulbs of Gin Lane.

In the core, the creature flinched, as if she had heard every one.

Alice raised her gauntlet and blew the door. BOOM. The glass held. She hit it again. It cracked. Tamara grabbed the creature and dragged her toward the outfall.

Alice turned back and held out her hand.

"Nathaniel. Come on."

He stayed where he was, by the pit, by his father.

"I need to hear the rest."

She looked at him for one second.

Then she left him there.

* * *

On Westminster Bridge, glass rained from the lamp posts onto the festival crowd. Then there was only the fireworks for light. Then a panicking crowd, ten thousand strong, packed shoulder to shoulder on a narrow bridge in the dark.

And then, the crush.

Cole's squad held a barricade at the north end with flash-bursts, FZZT, FZZT, trying to slow the surge. People still died against the railings. People died with their faces pressed to the iron.

Lord Lambrick stood up on a coal cart at the end of the bridge with his arms spread wide and roared over the screaming.

"Remember who kept you warm!"

* * *

In the core, after, it was very quiet.

Frost was melting off the walls in long slow drips. Morgana hung in her hooks with her head bowed, breathing.

Father and son stood alone with her.

"She said Jamie isn't dead," Nathaniel said. "She said he's cold."

Eldgard didn't look at Morgana when he answered.

"She tells every Harding whatever will bring him to the lock. She told me my sister was still singing." He said it like a man pressing on an old scar.

Then he turned to his son. "Thirty-three days. I won't lose two sons. Help me find another way."

Nathaniel couldn't tell if any of it was a lie.

He said yes.

He chose his father. And he put the word cold away, in the same place he kept the weeping in the floor.

* * *

On a rooftop over Gin Lane, Alice and the creature sat soaked and shaking in the black city.

The creature's hands were burned raw where the mittens had been. She held them in her lap and looked out at London, where the fireworks were still going up over a city with no lights.

"I'm going to free her," she said. "Even if all of them freeze."

Alice took her burned hand anyway, and held it.

Below them London lay lit in patches, every third lamp a broken stump, and far off down the river the last rockets of Lantern Night burst green over the water, and nobody was cheering.
