Episode Two. The Wake.

Forty-seven days to first snow.

* * *

The thing in Miriam's body sat up on the slab and screamed.

It was not a human sound. It was the sound of a pane of glass splitting from corner to corner in deep winter. Eeeeiiii, KKK.

Two morgue attendants froze where they stood. Not in fear. In frost. One had his foot lifted for a step, and it stayed lifted, white rime crawling up his trouser leg, his mouth open on a shout that never came. Felix Hawke's hand went numb on his revolver, and the gun dropped out of fingers he could no longer feel.

The girl swung her bare legs off the slab, looked at all of them with eyes that did not quite know what eyes were for, and ran. Out through the swinging doors, barefoot, into the night.

Alice ran after her.

* * *

The trail was frost. A white footprint on a wet cobble. A fern of ice on a gutter. A rime of white along a roof-ridge where something had climbed.

Alice followed it up a drainpipe and across the roofs of Gin Lane, slipping on slates, her apron torn, her breath tearing in her chest. The city was still half dark from the blackout, and the trail glowed faintly in it, like a snail's.

She found her sister curled inside a lamp tower.

The tower was one of the old iron ones at the end of the lane, and the creature wearing Miriam had climbed up into its glass head and wrapped herself round the gallowglass crystal like a cat round a stove. Her hands were cupped over it. As Alice watched, the crystal dimmed, and dimmed, its light draining into those blue-white fingers. The thing was drinking it. Getting warm.

"Mim," Alice whispered. "Mim. It's me."

The face turned. Miriam's face, Miriam's freckles, Miriam's grey eyes, and nothing in them that knew her.

So Alice did the only thing she could think of. She sat down on the cold iron platform and began to count, softly, the way they had since they were small.

"One post. Two post. Three post. Four."

A long silence. Then, in Miriam's voice, slow and strange, as if the words were coming up from the bottom of a well:

"Five post. Six post."

The body knew it, even if the girl inside it didn't. Alice put her face in her hands and wept.

* * *

Before dawn, she hammered on Old Wren's door.

Old Wren was the herb-woman of Gin Lane, sixty-odd, round as a cottage loaf and about as soft, with a brown face creased like a walnut, white hair tied up in a red kerchief, and forearms like a washerwoman's under rolled sleeves. Her room smelled of camphor and boiled cabbage and something sweet that hung in bunches from the ceiling. She had set Alice's arm when Alice was nine years old, and she had never once asked how it broke.

She laid the girl on her own bed and put her ear to the chest. She listened for a long time. The heart was there, but barely, slow as a clock running down. She held a hand mirror under the girl's lips, and the breath that came out barely fogged it.

Alice watched her face. Alice's own face was begging.

"Ice does this," Wren said at last. "I've seen a drowned boy sit up after an hour by the fire. She's had a shock, love. The memories come back in their own time."

She saw more than she said. Alice saw her see it, and asked her, with every line of her body, not to say it.

Wren didn't.

Alice took her sister home, and chose to believe.

* * *

In the parlour of Harding House, Jamie lay in a closed coffin of black walnut with silver handles.

The curtains were drawn. The mirrors were covered in crepe. The green lamps had been turned down so low the room looked like the bottom of a pond.

"I want to see him," Nathaniel said.

"No." Eldgard did not look up from the letters on his desk.

"He's my brother."

"He fell four hundred feet onto iron. You will remember him as he was."

Nathaniel put his hand on the coffin lid. Byron came up beside him and, very gently, moved the hand away. As he did it, Nathaniel noticed that the old man's own hands were shaking.

Then his mother was in front of him. Josephine took his face in her two gloved hands, the only time in all those weeks she would touch him without a reason, and looked into his eyes.

"You're what's left," she said. "You will stay out of that place."

It wasn't a request. The kid leather against his cheeks was cold.

* * *

At the Lantern Guard tower, Nixon sat at his new captain's desk and wrote his first report.

Saboteur in the core. Engaged by the Guard. Fled in the dark.

He signed it. His hand did not shake. He was quite proud of that.

Cole Pruett came in with the tea, cheerful as a sparrow. "I saw you hit him, sir. Flash went right through him."

Nixon did not look up. "You saw a flash, Wick. Flashes lie."

Cole thought about that, and decided to believe him, because believing in his captain was the best thing in his life.

But that night, in his bunk, by a stub of candle, he wrote all of it down in his diary anyway. The flash. The shoulder. And the man on the floor, still talking, when the Captain was sent away.

* * *

In the Gin Lane tenement, two rooms up four flights of stairs that smelled of cats and gin and boiled bones, the thing that was not Miriam met the world.

It met bread, and turned the heel over and over in its mittened hands as if it were a jewel.

It met a kettle. The kettle began to whistle, eeeEEEEE, and the creature flung itself flat behind the bed and would not come out until Alice took the kettle off the hob.

It met a cat. A scrawny tabby with one ear, who belonged to nobody and came in through the window when it liked. The creature looked at the cat, and the cat looked at the creature, and something opened in Miriam's face like a door. Delight. Pure and simple, the delight of a child.

"Ali," it said, exactly the way Miriam used to. "Ali, look."

It picked the cat up.

The cat went stiff. Its fur turned white. Its eyes glazed over. In four heartbeats it was frozen solid in her arms, a small grey statue with its mouth open.

The creature stared at it. It did not understand. It shook the cat gently, the way you'd shake a sleeper, and the cat didn't wake.

Alice understood. She took the cat out of her sister's arms and laid it in a box by the stove, and she told herself it was the cold, still in Mim from the bridge, just the way Wren had said. It would pass.

So, rules. Gloves at all times. No touching. Not until it passed.

Miriam's red mittens went on her hands.

"You'll be yourself again soon, Mim."

The creature looked at her a long moment. A thing from somewhere else, deciding whether to tell the truth to the only warm thing in the world.

"Yes, Ali."

The first lie. And the first kindness.

* * *

In the Lambrick coach, rolling through Mayfair, the coal baron lit a cigar and looked at his daughter.

"The heir is dead," Lambrick said. "Your fiancé is the heir now. Marry him fast."

"Let me run the books instead," said Tamara.

"You're a wedding, not a ledger."

She folded her hands in her lap and said nothing. But his account book lay open on the seat beside him, and Tamara Lambrick could read a ledger upside down at a glance. Her eyes went down the column. Coal. Coal. Coal. And one line that was not coal.

Second of November. Works gate. Two hundred guineas. A.F.

She looked out the window and memorised it.

* * *

St Bride's church was packed for the funeral, and every lamp in it burned green.

The Lantern Guard formed the honour guard in their bottle-green coats. Nixon carried the coffin with five others, and it was wrong in his hands. It was heavier than a man. And on the steps, when they tilted it, nothing inside it settled. It was dead-still, like a box of stones. He told himself that was what guilt weighed.

The great houses of London filled the pews. In the front sat Lord Chancellor Vane, sixty, a long dry stick of a man in black silk with a face like folded parchment, a gold pince-nez on a ribbon, and the patient, bored expression of a man who had attended a great many funerals and expected to attend a great many more. He represented the Crown. He did not, as a rule, ask questions.

Nathaniel stood up to give the eulogy. He got halfway through. Then he stopped, and stood there with the paper shaking in his hand, and couldn't go on.

The organ began the Lantern Hymn, the old tune every London child knew, and the church lamps flared brighter with it. Under the hymn, Nathaniel heard it again. The weeping. The same tune, a half-step down, like a woman singing it from under the floor.

He looked away, and his eyes landed on the chancel wall, where the Harding brasses hung. Plate after plate of dead Hardings. And among them, one small one. A girl's.

Ottoline Harding. Eighteen twenty to eighteen thirty-nine. Died abroad.

He leaned toward Byron. "Who was she?"

"Your father's sister, sir."

Byron said nothing more. In the front pew, Eldgard never once turned his head toward the plate.

Tamara reached over and took Nathaniel's hand. Kindness, nothing more.

Across the aisle, Nixon watched.

* * *

At the graveside, a fine rain was falling.

Alice was there in a borrowed black coat, posing as a mourner from the Works. She was there for her contract. And she was there for herself, because her sister had loved this dead man, and someone from Gin Lane ought to stand at his grave.

Nathaniel recognised the lamp-girl. He came and stood beside her while the earth went in. Thud. Thud. Two people grieving, and one of them lying.

He took out his handkerchief and held it out to her. White linen, his initials in the corner in grey thread. It was the only thing he had done all day that nobody had told him to do.

She took it. She kept it.

* * *

In the Shadwell laundry, among the steam and the hanging sheets, a veiled woman sat down across the counting table from Mother Grise and laid a purse of gold on the wood.

Clink.

"For the contract I placed," Josephine Harding said, lifting her veil. "Paid in full. And I'm doubling it."

Grise counted the coins without looking at them. "The elder boy's dead."

"Which is why I'm doubling it. Keep him close. Keep him out of the Works." Josephine's voice did not change. "His father will use him."

So it had been Nathaniel's own mother, all along, who paid a Gin Lane girl to get close to her son. And Josephine Harding, it turned out, knew her way around a Vykra laundry. She had sat at that table before. Long ago.

* * *

That night, Nathaniel forced the outer gate of the Works.

He went down through the dark gantries with a hand-lamp, past the vats, past the great valves, down and down toward the core. The core door was sealed. His father's men were standing in front of it, and they turned him back, politely, firmly, a hand on each elbow.

But on the stairs, going back up, his lamp caught a glint of brass on an iron step.

He picked it up. A spent cartridge. Lantern Guard issue, for a lantern rifle.

He turned it over in his fingers for a long time. His brother had not fallen. Somebody had fired.

* * *

On the roof of the Guard tower, in the wind, Nathaniel held the cartridge up between two fingers.

"This is one of yours."

Nixon looked at it. "Half the corps was down there after."

"Then find me the one who was down there before."

Nixon held out his hand. Nathaniel dropped the cartridge into it.

And so Captain Nixon Hargrave was now investigating himself.

* * *

In the tenement, the creature kept drawing on the window.

Every time frost formed on the pane, which was often now, she drew in it with one mittened finger. A chain. Always a chain. Link after link after link. And under her breath, the same word.

"Mother."

Alice wiped it away, gently, with her sleeve. "Mum's gone, Mim. Ten years."

The creature let her think that was who she meant.

Then Alice, untangling the cord of the red mittens from around her sister's neck, found what else hung on it. A ring. A man's signet ring, gold, cut with a Harding crest.

"Jamie's," Alice said.

The creature looked down at it. Something moved in Miriam's face that was not quite hers. "He was supposed to come."

So Alice needed to know why he hadn't.

* * *

She broke into Harding House that same night, Vykra-quiet, over the garden wall and up the wisteria and through a sash window into Jamie Harding's bedroom.

Somebody was already there.

A shape in the dark, searching the drawers. She had a knife out before her feet touched the floor. He had a brass lamp-iron raised before he turned round. Then a sliver of moonlight crossed both their faces.

"You," he said.

"You," she said.

A standoff in the dark. And it ended, as such things sometimes do, with the truth. Nearly.

"My sister loved your brother," Alice said. "She nearly died the same night he did."

Nathaniel lowered the iron. He hadn't known Jamie loved anyone.

Then the door handle turned.

There was one place to go. Jamie's wardrobe. They went into it together, among his coats, chest to chest in a dark that smelled of cedar and cologne and dead man, and pulled the door to. Nathaniel's heart was going against her shoulder like a fist on a door.

Through the slats, they watched Eldgard Harding come in alone with a candle.

He sat on his dead son's bed. He took a pocket watch out of his waistcoat, Jamie's watch, the one left on his desk the night of the ball, and he wound it.

Tk, tk, tk, tk, tk.

He didn't say a word. It was worse than anything he could have said.

Nathaniel's breath caught. He moved, about to push the door open, about to step out and go to his father. Alice put her hand over his mouth.

He went still.

The candle went out of the room, and down the back stair, toward the Works.

She didn't take her hand away fast enough. Neither of them mentioned it.

Crouched among Jamie's shoes, they found what they had both come for. A letter, rolled tight and pushed inside a wooden boot-tree. An escape plan, for two. And a diagram, in Jamie's hand, of a great chain and a lock. Beside it, a second stair: thirteen steps, going down from a hatch marked coal.

At the window, going, there was a beat where neither of them knew what to say.

Then she gave him one true thing.

"Bounds."

"And the rest of it?"

"Girls on Gin Lane don't give the rest of it to a Harding."

So she was Bounds to him. It was the only name he would use for her all season, first as a joke and then as something he was afraid to change.

She dropped from the sill into the garden. Below the window, Byron, passing along the terrace with a candle, watched her land and roll and run. He said nothing. He smiled.

* * *

Far below the Works, Eldgard stood alone in the core with the chained light.

It was all green glow and shadow down there, and the vast patient breathing of something enormous, and the voice that wept.

"He was mine," Eldgard said, "and I spent him."

He held up his lamp to the chain. The links nearest the lock were frayed, iron worn thin as wire. He counted the days in his head.

"Forty-seven."

* * *

At Scotland Yard, Felix Hawke sat at a desk under a single green lamp with his frostbitten hand in a sling.

He had two frozen attendants. He had a missing corpse. He had newspapers that had already given the thing a name: the Frost Killer. And he had an order on his desk from the Crown, telling him to drop the case, with Eldgard Harding's influence all over it like fingerprints in soot.

He put the order in a drawer. He kept the file. He went walking.

On Gin Lane, an old man selling hot potatoes told him what he'd seen. A girl with frost on her hands, on the Blackwood stair. "Bounds girl. Lives on the Lane with her sister."

Felix climbed the four flights and knocked.

Alice was out. The door opened, and Miriam Bounds, who had been dead on his slab two nights before, stood there in red mittens, frightened as a deer.

Frost bloomed across the door. Krrrk.

Felix's good hand, resting on the frame, froze to the wood.

She bolted past him, down the stairs, and was gone.

* * *

Alice came home to a frozen door and a policeman on her landing with both hands bandaged.

"Your sister was dead," Felix said. "The surgeon said she was with child. Then she got up. Explain that to me."

"Your surgeon was wrong," Alice said. "The cold fools them. They pull men out of the Thames in January who sit up on the slab."

She believed every word. That was why Felix couldn't tell if she was lying.

He didn't arrest her. He filed the word child away. So did she.

That night she asked Old Wren, once.

Wren, who'd had her ear to that chest for an hour, looked at the fire. "If there was a child, love, the cold took it. Let it go."

Alice let it go. She didn't say the word again.

It was Wren's second kind lie. And the bigger one.

* * *

On Tyburn Road, the creature walked alone.

She had heard the weeping again, and followed it, all the way across the dark city to the iron gates of the Harding Works. She stood there in the drizzle and laid one red-mittened palm flat on the gate.

Every gas-lamp on the street bent its flame toward her. All of them, at once, like flowers turning to the sun.

Up on the wall, on night watch, Captain Nixon Hargrave saw it.

* * *

Far below, in the core, the chain jerked.

Eldgard was standing at the lock with his lamp. He felt it in the floor, a shiver, as if something enormous had drawn breath.

For the first time in four hundred years, the prisoner lifted her head.

And he saw her face. Blue ice, carved like a queen's, and in the eyes, green fire.

"My daughter," said Morgana, "is here."

Eldgard dropped his lamp. It hit the iron floor and rolled, and the light went spinning round the walls.
