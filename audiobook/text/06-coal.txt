Episode Six. Coal.

Twenty-eight days to first snow.

* * *

Thirty years earlier. The core gate. Night watch.

Sergeant Byron of the Royal Lantern Guard was thirty-four years old, broad-shouldered and black-moustached, in a green coat with three brass chevrons on the sleeve. He stood his post at the iron gate above the core stair with a lantern rifle across his chest, and he listened to the weeping, because on night watch there was nothing else to listen to.

Then a shadow moved at the end of the corridor.

He caught her before she reached the study door. A girl, nineteen, in a black dress cut for running, with dark gold hair pinned tight and a long thin knife in her hand. Sent by somebody, for somebody. For young Eldgard Harding, asleep upstairs.

He caught her because she had stopped.

She had been halfway past the core gate when the weeping came up the stair, and she had stopped, and listened. Just a breath too long. Her face in the green light was wide open, as if somebody had called her name.

Byron put the rifle under her chin. She looked at him. He looked at her.

And he let her go.

A year later, Josephine married the man she had been sent to kill. Byron served at the wedding, in white gloves, carrying the cake.

In the pocket of his tunic, that day, was a faded blue ribbon. It was from a different night, twenty years before, when he was a boy of fourteen.

* * *

Since Lantern Night, half the lamps in London were broken glass, and the rest came and went like a dying man's breath.

The cold had come in. Not snow. Just cold, hard and grey and bone-deep, settling into the city like water into a cellar.

And with it came the coal carts. Big black wagons with LAMBRICK painted on their sides in red, pulled by steaming dray horses, rolling down every street. On the corners, men chalked up prices on boards. Doubled. Then tripled. People queued in the dark with buckets.

In a doorway on Gin Lane, a child was asleep. A little girl, six or seven, curled up in a ragged shawl, with frost in her eyelashes. A constable walked past and looked away.

It was Alice's doorway.

By morning, the child wasn't asleep.

* * *

At a workbench in the Harding Works, father and son were building something together.

They called it the Heartglass. A resonance cage, Eldgard said, all curved crystal ribs and brass, meant to hold the chain in tune without a Harding death. Nathaniel drew. Eldgard corrected. Nathaniel corrected him back. They finished each other's equations. Eldgard laughed once, at something his son said, a real laugh, and put a hand on his shoulder.

It was everything Nathaniel had ever wanted.

"We'll need something of her blood to tune it," Eldgard said.

Nathaniel nodded and wrote it down. He assumed his father meant Morgana.

* * *

In a borrowed room on Gin Lane, two floors above a cobbler's, the creature sat on the bed with her burned hands in new mittens. Grey ones. Not red.

Wren had said the memories would come back. They were going the other way.

Every time the cold came out of her, it cost one of Miriam's memories. And tonight, the creature looked up at Alice and asked, quite innocently:

"When's your birthday, Ali?"

Alice stopped, with the kettle in her hand.

The same day Miriam had forgotten it. The same day this creature had remembered it, with an orange.

"You knew it once," Alice said.

The creature's face went frightened. She reached out and grabbed Alice's sleeve. "There's something I have to say to you before it goes. About him. About the belt."

"After."

"There might not be an after."

"After, Mim."

The fence held. The creature watched the thing she needed to say slipping further away, like a boat drifting off a pier.

And then she went harder, because she was frightened. "If I free my mother, I'll be strong again."

"If you free her, the light goes out and people die."

"People die now. I watched one on your step."

Silence.

Then, quieter: "At the chain, it all came back. Your birthday. The songs. For one second I had all of her. If I could sit by her an hour, I'd have Mim back for you."

It was the only offer anyone had made Alice since the bridge.

"You sit by her," Alice said. "You don't touch the lock."

"Yes, Ali."

The second lie. And this time, Alice half knew it.

* * *

At midnight, Alice climbed the wall of the Harding Works.

She wanted to tell the only person who would understand that her sister had forgotten her birthday. She went up the brickwork like a cat, along a gutter, to the lit window of the gauntlet shop.

Through the glass she saw Nathaniel and Eldgard, still at one bench, heads together over the drawings. His father's hand was on his shoulder. The Heartglass stood half-built between them, glittering.

It was everything he had wanted his whole life. She could see that it was.

She didn't knock.

Her breath fogged the pane. She wiped it clean with her glove, the glove with his handkerchief folded inside it, and went back down the wall.

A second later, Nathaniel looked up, at a window with nobody in it.

* * *

In the gun room at Lambrick House, Tamara stood behind a velvet curtain and did not breathe.

Her father was at the table with Ajax Fink. A map lay between them, and she could see it through the gap in the curtain. The Tyburn conduit, the old Works drain, running under Pimlico. And where it met the Lambrick gas main, a cluster of little red crosses.

"Charges along the conduit," Lambrick said. "Where it meets my main. On the night of the vote. The Harding Works blow out their own drain, and the Crown sees how rotten they are."

Ajax crunched his apple. "And if your daughter gets in the way?"

A pause.

"Then my daughter should have stayed home."

Then Lambrick turned a page in his ledger, and said, not to Ajax, but to the ledger, mildly, as if he were noting the price of coke: "And if she comes home and talks, Dr Cutler has signed the papers. I only need a second doctor."

Behind the curtain, Tamara learned her price twice in one breath.

* * *

In a Covent Garden tea shop, all steamed windows and wicker chairs and the smell of toasted teacake, Alice Bounds swallowed her pride and sat down across from Tamara Lambrick.

She needed a way into the core. Tamara was the new Vykra with a coal baron's maps.

Tamara told her about the charges.

"The only route into the core runs through my father's tunnels," Tamara said. "Ajax will be in them. Go another night."

"There isn't one," said Alice. "She's forgetting me."

Two girls who disliked each other, starting to understand each other.

The girl who poured their tea was small and quiet, with mouse-brown hair and a burn scar on her wrist. Stitched inside her glove was a Vykra knot.

Neither of them looked at her hands.

* * *

Under a bridge on the frozen canal at Little Venice, Nathaniel met Alice in secret.

There was no snow yet, only cold, iron-hard. The canal was frozen grey, and narrowboats were locked in the ice along the towpath, their chimneys smoking. Their breath smoked between them in the dark under the arch.

He told her about the Heartglass. "We can save both of them. Your sister and me. No Tithe. No breaking."

She told him the creature was forgetting her.

She pulled off a glove to warm her hands with her breath, and something fell out of it onto the frost. A white handkerchief, folded very small. His initials in grey thread. Kept where a Vykra keeps the thing that says whose she is.

He picked it up.

"You kept it."

"Bounds keeps things."

And for once in his life he didn't wait to be reached for. He kissed her.

He found out that she had been waiting since the ballroom.

Above them, ice ticked off the bridge. Tik. Tik. Tik.

And then both of them used the kiss to hide the dangerous part. She was going in tonight. And he still didn't know what the Heartglass was for.

* * *

In the linen room at Harding House, among the shelves of folded sheets and the smell of lavender and starch, Byron shut the door behind him.

"I saw your hands," he said.

Josephine didn't turn round. "I did it for Nathaniel."

"You always say that."

She had a needle in her sleeve. She could feel it there, against her wrist.

She didn't use it.

"Thirty years, Josie," Byron said, and left.

She sat down on a stack of linen and did not cry.

Then a maid knocked and brought her a folded note in Grise's hand, bought an hour ago from a tea-girl in Covent Garden.

Tyburn conduit. Vote night. The girl.

Josephine took it straight down to her husband's study and laid it on his desk.

"How do you know?"

"I'm your wife."

Eldgard sent for Nixon. When the captain came, he didn't ask him to sit.

"You stood at my core door and let her walk out of it. Bring her back alive, Captain. Or bring me your lantern."

The Heartglass needed her.

And the woman who had just spared Byron's life had set the trap he would die in. Neither of them knew it.

* * *

A cipher note reached Nathaniel at his bench, slipped under the shop door. It was in his and Tamara's childhood code, the one they had invented at twelve to pass notes across their parents' dinner tables.

The conduit. Charges. She's going in.

He ran, grabbing the leap-gauntlet as he went.

In the front hall of Harding House, Byron saw him tear out across the yard.

Byron stood very still. Then he walked to the wall, where his old Lantern hung in a glass case, his Guard lamp from thirty years ago. He opened the case and took it down. It was still charged.

When it lit, he heard her again. As clearly as he had on night watch. The weeping, low and patient, coming up out of the green.

He followed anyway.

* * *

The Tyburn conduit, under Pimlico, was warm and wet and black.

Alice and the creature went down into it. In a side drain, Ajax's men were crouched over a line of fuses, lighting their pipes. In a warm nook beyond them, where the gas main ran hot through the brick, four tunnel children were sleeping in a heap of rags.

Ahead, in the dark, Nixon's squad was waiting. Cole was among them.

Cole leaned close to his captain and held up his diary in the dark, its pages pale.

"Tell Mister Harding, sir. Or I will."

Nixon looked at the diary.

"After the vote."

Cole agreed. Because he still believed in him.

* * *

Flash-bursts. FZZT. FZZT.

The tunnel froze into pictures. Alice's raised gauntlet. The creature's white face. Nixon, lunging.

He cornered the creature against the brick, and she grabbed his bare wrist.

No frost.

He didn't freeze. The way Nathaniel hadn't. And in the strobe and the shouting, nobody saw it but the two of them.

She went white anyway. She could smell Harding on him, the way she had smelled it on Nathaniel, and under it, something else. A cellar. The cellar under her mother. And one of the names in the song she had heard in every lamp since the bridge, a name the lamps had been singing for twenty years, belonged to the same blood as his.

"They told you your mother had a fever," she said. "She didn't."

He let go of her as if she had burned him.

Then Alice was on him. Gauntlet to gauntlet, in a tunnel too narrow to swing in, shoulder against brick, elbow against jaw. The first real fight between them. Neither of them wanted to win.

Then Nathaniel came down the tunnel at a dead run, screaming.

"Gas! Get out! Gas!"

Ajax saw the Guard, and lit early.

And the creature's cold met a flash-burst in the gas main.

* * *

The Pimlico gasworks erupted.

WHOOOOM.

Fire rolled out along the river in a wall, orange and black, lighting up both banks.

A mile downriver, in the Palace of Westminster, the Charter vote was three names from done. Eldgard in his place. Lambrick in his. Vane presiding. Nathaniel's chair, empty. And Josephine, in the gallery, looking at it.

Every window in the chamber blew in on the voting lords.

* * *

In the rubble, in the smoke, a green flash. Then another.

Byron's Lantern, searching. Finding the boy.

Nathaniel lay pinned under a fallen iron beam, half buried in brick, coughing, his leg trapped. Byron got his shoulder under the beam and lifted it. Took the weight himself. His whole body shook with it.

Nathaniel crawled free.

The beam came down on Byron, and it crushed him.

Nathaniel scrambled back to him, clawing at the bricks. The old man lay on his back in the dust with his Lantern beside him, still glowing green.

Dying, he said: "Your mother killed the inspector. She does it for you. Don't let her."

A breath. Then the thing he had known for thirty years. "I let her go at the gate. Everything since came through that gate."

Nathaniel held his hand.

"You were always enough," said Byron.

He died with his hand on the boy's face.

In his other fist, when Nathaniel finally opened it, was a faded blue ribbon.

* * *

On the river stair, Nixon came up out of the fire with Cole Pruett over his shoulders.

Behind him, Alice came up with the creature. The creature turned at the top of the stair and looked back down into the burning tunnel, where four children were trapped in their nook behind a wall of flame. She raised both hands, and she used the last of her cold on it.

The fire went out with a long hiss, like a held breath let go. Hhhhhhhhh.

The four children came stumbling up the stair in their rags, alive, black with soot, crying.

Then the creature turned and looked at Alice. Politely. The way you'd look at a stranger who had helped you up from a fall.

"Thank you, miss."

She didn't know her name.

Alice told herself it was the shock again. Worse this time.

It was the last time she would manage to.

* * *

In the Palace of Westminster, glass crunched underfoot. The chamber was full of smoke and cold air pouring through the empty windows.

Lord Chancellor Vane stood at the dispatch box with blood running from a cut on his temple, and announced, in a voice that didn't shake at all, that the Harding Charter was suspended. The Works would pass to a board appointed by the Crown.

Lord Lambrick took a seat on it.

Eldgard Harding had lost the Works.

* * *

Nathaniel walked into the music room at Harding House covered in ash.

His mother was at the piano, playing something slow, a nocturne. The room was lit by candles. She looked up.

"Byron's dead," he said.

She wept. Really wept, her hands going still on the keys, her shoulders shaking.

He waited until she was done.

"He told me about Inspector Hawke."

The music had already stopped.

A long beat. The candles guttered. Somewhere in the house a clock ticked.

Josephine wiped her eyes with one gloved finger, and looked at her son.

"Then you know I love you."
