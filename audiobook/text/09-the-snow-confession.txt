Episode Nine. The Snow Confession.

First snow. Dusk to midnight.

* * *

Twenty years earlier. The Serpentine, frozen.

The ice was blue-white under a low winter sun, and the fashionable world went round and round on it, and in the middle of it all, a man in a black greatcoat was teaching a little boy to skate.

The boy was five, fair-haired, round-cheeked, bundled in a red muffler, and he fell. And fell. And fell. Every time, he landed on his bottom with a whump and laughed, and every time, his father laughed too, and hauled him up by the armpits, and set him on his feet again. Eldgard Harding laughing. Few people alive had seen it.

Then the boy fell one more time, and Eldgard knelt down on the ice in front of him, and took his small mittened hands in his.

"One day," he said, "the city will need you to be very brave."

Jamie looked up at him, rosy and solemn. "I'll be brave, Papa."

Eldgard's face, as he heard it.

On the bank, Josephine stood holding the boy's scarf, a long red scarf he had pulled off because it itched. She didn't take her eyes off him. Not once.

* * *

Twenty years later, snow was falling through the broken panes of the Harding glasshouse.

It drifted down between the orange trees. It settled on the workbench, on the cold stove, on the frost-written words on the glass by the door. Don't follow me.

Nathaniel said it first, because he had had all afternoon to look at that glass.

"You nodded. And I said it. And while we were saying it, they took her."

"She'd have been in Bethlem by supper," said Alice.

"Your sister—"

"Sent me up there. She'd already decided. She was going, Nathaniel. I only chose who got saved on the way."

He owed her something he could never pay back. Both of them knew it. And it made him cruel for exactly one line.

"My mother's receipt had your name on it. That's where I learned it. On a bill."

Alice didn't flinch. That was how he knew it had gone in.

Then she gave him the rest of the sentence from that morning. All of it. The way she had given her sister fifteen years in one breath.

"At first I was paid. Then you flew across that foundry into a coal bin for an audience of one, and you looked at me like I was the first person who'd ever watched. After that, I'd have done it for nothing." A breath. "I did do it for nothing. Grise cut me loose a month ago, and I kept coming."

Snow ticked on the broken glass. Tik. Tik.

"I'm married," he said.

"I know. I nodded."

He reached for her across the workbench. She let him get halfway. Then she stood up.

"Midnight. The core. After that, we'll see what either of us is allowed."

For the first time, it was her plan, and he followed it.

Midnight at the core. They needed the wolf-crest ring, the one Alistair had closed the chain with four hundred years ago. It was on Eldgard's hand, in a Crown cell.

And they needed Nixon.

* * *

The old bell foundry in Whitechapel was a cavern of soot and rust, its great casting pits empty, a cracked bell the size of a cottage lying on its side in the corner.

Forty disgraced Lanterns were cooking sausages on flash-gauntlets set to low. Hssss. Spit. Hssss. The smell of burning fat filled the air. Their green coats were torn and muddy. None of them had a lantern on his collar.

Nathaniel walked in out of the snow.

Every man stood up.

Nixon stood in the middle of them, a sausage on a fork. They looked at each other across the foundry.

"You shot my brother," said Nathaniel.

"Yes."

"My father did the rest. I think."

Silence. The sausages spat.

"What do you need?" said Nixon.

"Newgate. Then the Works."

Nixon put down his fork. "You know we're cousins? Cadet line."

Nathaniel looked at him for a long moment. "Then you're family. God help you."

* * *

At Lambrick House, the coal fires roared, and the coal baron stood in front of one of them with his hands behind his back and spoke to his daughter.

"If the girl fails, the Crown needs a Harding by midnight. And it has to walk down on its own feet. Your father-in-law would hang first. The boy will walk if he thinks it saves somebody." He turned. "You'll bring your husband."

Then, kindly: "Bring him, and I'll burn the order at Bethlem. Don't, and remember that a widow's nearest relative is her father again."

"Yes, Father," said Tamara.

He believed her. Because he had never once looked at her.

An hour later, in his study, she knelt in front of his safe for the last time. Tick. Tick. Tick-tick. Click. She took the Works master key, a great iron thing on a ring. And this time, she took the ledger itself. The whole book. Every page. Six months of payments to A.F., all the way down to the night of the Charter vote.

A.F. Pimlico. Three hundred guineas.

She turned around.

Ajax Fink was in the doorway, with an apple.

This time she didn't freeze. She held up a needle, slim and silver, Vykra issue, the one she had picked up on the gallery stairs the day Felix Hawke died.

"Try it," she said.

Ajax looked at the needle. He looked at her. And he smiled, and stepped aside.

Then he went to tell her father.

* * *

Snow lay on Gin Lane, grey with soot, and the gutters were frozen.

On her way to take the Vykra, Alice stopped at Old Wren's door. Because there might not be another chance.

Wren opened it in her red kerchief, with a shawl around her shoulders, and looked at Alice's face, and didn't ask her in.

"You knew," Alice said. "The first night. You held the mirror under her mouth, and you knew."

Wren didn't deny it. "I knew you couldn't hear it yet. Five weeks you had a sister. I'd give them to you again."

"And the child?"

Wren was quiet for a moment. Snow fell between them.

"Still there," she said. "Cold holds what it holds. It held her heart still on that bridge, and it's holding the baby the same way. I didn't know if either would ever start again. So I told you it was gone."

Alice couldn't decide whether to thank her or hate her. There wasn't time to decide.

Wren reached out and touched Alice's arm. The left one. The one she had set, when Alice was nine.

"Nobody stood in front of you then, either," she said. "Go on."

Alice went.

* * *

In the Shadwell laundry, the coppers were boiling, and steam hung in the air like fog, and long white sheets hung from the drying lines in rows, so that the whole hall was a maze of linen.

Mother Grise sat in her high chair by the counting table.

"I sold your sister," she said, "because she's worth more than you."

Alice stood in front of her with the snow melting on her shoulders.

"Then I call challenge. By Vykra law."

The whole laundry went silent. Forty women stopped work.

Grise looked at her for a long moment. Then she got down from her chair, slowly, the old bones creaking, and took a knife from her sleeve.

They fought among the sheets.

Fwip. Fwip. Two shadows through linen. A blade slicing a sheet in half. A shape lunging. A shape twisting away.

Grise was seventy, and she was the best knife in London. She was everywhere. Alice was bleeding from the arm, the cheek, the hand.

So Alice did the only thing she knew. She counted.

Under her breath, the way Miriam had taught her under the bed. The count she used to hide inside.

One post. Two post. Three post. Four.

Grise's knife hissed past her ear.

Five post. Six post. Seven.

Grise came in low.

Eight.

And on nine, Alice moved.

Nine. The count the Lost One had given her. The one where you get up.

She was inside Grise's guard before the old woman could turn, and her knife was at Grise's throat, and Grise's back was against a boiling copper.

They stood like that for a long moment, breathing.

Then Alice lowered the knife.

She spared the old woman.

All around the laundry, the Vykra girls picked up their knives.

"Where?" said one.

"Tyburn," said Alice.

* * *

In the snowlit hall of Harding House, Josephine sat alone beside Byron's empty chair.

Snow light came in through the tall windows, blue and cold. Nobody had lit the lamps. There were no lamps left to light.

She got up and went to a cabinet in the corner, and from behind a false back she took out a long flat box of black lacquer. She hadn't opened it in thirty years.

Inside, on faded velvet, lay six Vykra knives.

She took off her rings. She buckled the knives on, one by one. Wrist. Wrist. Thigh. Thigh. Back. Boot.

* * *

The yard of Newgate Prison was a well of black stone with snow falling into it.

Then, from over the walls, kshhh, THOOM. Kshhh, THOOM. Kshhh, THOOM.

Nixon's forty came over the walls on leap-boosters, green coats against the snow. Flash-bursts strobed the yard. FZZT. FZZT. Crown guards stumbled blind.

Nathaniel landed at Eldgard's cell window and cut the bars with his gauntlet. The iron screamed and glowed and parted.

Eldgard looked up from his plank bed. "You came for me."

"I came for your ring."

Two cells down, Nixon quietly opened fourteen-seventeen as well.

Old Tobias Marsh shuffled out into the yard in his filthy blanket, looked up at the snow falling out of the dark sky, and held out his palm to catch it. And he spoke, perfectly lucid.

"They always count from the first snow."

At the main gate, Cole Pruett persuaded a turnkey to unlock it with extreme politeness and a lantern rifle.

"If you'd be so kind, sir. Thank you, sir. Lovely evening, sir."

* * *

On the Newgate rooftops, Crown guards were firing.

Eldgard couldn't leap. He was fifty-eight and he had been in a cell for twelve hours and his knees were bad.

So Nixon carried him. Over his shoulder, like a sack of coal, across the snowy slates, under fire. The spare, carrying the man who bred him.

"Thank you, Captain," said Eldgard.

"Don't," said Nixon.

* * *

At the Works, the snow was thickening over Tyburn.

Where it lay deepest, against the walls, in the yard, in the ditch, something was happening to it. It was moving. Shaping. A muzzle. A shoulder. A pair of blue eyes, opening in a drift. Wolves, shaping themselves out of the snow.

On the gallery above the core, Lord Chancellor Vane stood with his hands in his pockets, nervous. In his breast pocket, against his heart, was a folded document with a broken seal. The sealed annex to the Harding Charter, signed in sixteen eighty-nine. The Crown's own signature on the chain. Every Lord Chancellor for two hundred years had kept it locked in his desk. Tonight, for reasons he couldn't have explained, Vane had taken it out.

Lambrick, beside him, checked his watch. "Midnight. The girl, or the boy."

Below them, in the Heartglass, cracks were spreading.

Every time the glass hummed toward the trade, toward putting the daughter in the mother's place, Morgana pulled against her own hooks. And another crack ran.

Tik. Tik.

"You were free," Morgana said, hoarse. "Why did you come back?"

"Because you're my mother," said the creature.

Morgana didn't answer.

Another crack ran.

And on Byron's old Guard stair, there were footsteps. Josephine was coming down.

* * *

At the outfall below the Works, in the snow, the whole young alliance stood together for the first time.

Alice and her Vykra, knives out, hoods up. Nixon and his forty Lanterns, rifles slung. Tamara Lambrick, Lady Harding, in a fur coat over her ruined wedding dress, with the master key in her hand. Nathaniel. And Eldgard, grey-faced, leaning on a rifle like a walking stick.

Cole sidled up to Tamara. "Begging your pardon, ma'am. Are married ladies allowed to fight?"

She looked at him. Then she reached into her coat and handed him a knife.

"Thank you, ma'am."

Then Tamara turned the key in the outfall gate. Clunk.

The Lanterns went over the walls. The Vykra went up the outfall.

* * *

The courtyard of the Works was a battlefield in the snow.

Three armies. Crown troops in red. Nixon's mutineers in green. Lambrick's coal-men in black, with clubs and shovels.

And then the wolves came out of the drifts, and attacked everyone.

Flash-bursts turned the snow green. FZZT. FZZT. FZZT. Every flash showed a wolf, a man, a blade, a mouth.

* * *

In the middle of the courtyard, Ajax Fink came out of the snow.

The Black Gauntlet swung. WHUNK.

It drove Nathaniel into the ground. He went down on his back in the snow, the wind knocked out of him, and Ajax stood over him, grinning. The scar from the Thames ran pink from brow to jaw.

"You should've pulled me up, Harding."

The fist went up for the killing blow.

And Eldgard Harding stepped under it.

WHUNK.

His ribs gave way. Crunch.

Alice, who had told Nathaniel to pull Ajax up, drove the man off with flash after flash. FZZT. FZZT. FZZT. Ajax staggered back, blind, and retreated, through a door, inside.

Inside was the core. And her sister. And the clock on the Works tower said twenty to twelve.

Alice had the charge left to chase him.

She looked at the door.

She looked at Nathaniel, on his knees in the snow, with his father across his lap.

She stayed.

* * *

Alice knelt behind Nathaniel, with her forehead against his shoulder blade, out of the conversation, spending minutes her sister didn't have.

Nathaniel held his father in the snow. And he asked the question from the dressing room again. Because this time there was no wedding to get through.

"Did you hold his hand?"

"I held his hand," said Eldgard.

A breath. Blood in it.

"Over the lock. Nixon's round went through his shoulder. He would have lived. I held his hand on the iron, until the crack he'd cut closed over."

Nathaniel didn't let go of him. That was the cruelty of it. He couldn't.

"It took all of him to mend it. I spent your brother on a crack in a lock."

"And then you filled his coffin with slag. I opened it."

"The chain leaves nothing to bury. It left nothing of Otty." Eldgard's eyes moved to Nixon. "The Captain carried it for me."

"She says he's cold," said Nathaniel. "Not dead."

On his father's other side, Nixon remembered the weight on the steps of St Bride's.

"Don't go looking for him, Nathaniel," Eldgard said. "That's how she gets a Harding to the lock. Hoping."

* * *

"When you were five—" Eldgard began.

"I know," said Nathaniel. "She told me I'd been down the stairs. I remembered the lantern on my own."

Eldgard almost laughed. There was blood in it. "Then she was kinder than I was."

He turned his head, slowly, to Nixon, kneeling at his other side.

"Cadet line. My spare. I took you from the Orchard at six. Your mother asked after you every day for four years, and I told her you were learning your letters."

Nixon's voice was very quiet. "I was."

"I know. I checked."

He meant it as love.

"The Orchard's children stopped walking in eighteen thirty-nine. So I raised one outside it. A Hargrave with a name and a rank and a family to love, who would walk down that stair for my son one night without being asked. I couldn't order it. An order would have spoiled you. I let you lock him in on Lantern Night, so you'd see what he was for."

It was the worst thing he said all night.

"Forgive me, or don't."

Nixon learned, in one breath, that he hadn't killed Jamie, and what he had been raised for. And he did neither.

"Jamie found your mother's bunk, Captain," Eldgard said. "He chalked his initials over that door, so the next one down would know somebody had seen it. Then he came up and asked me how many. I told him the truth, once. That was the night he stopped being my son and started being his own."

He turned back to Nathaniel.

"There was always a way out. Free her, and pay in light. Alistair chose sons. My father chose my sister. Otty. She was nineteen. She walked down the stair singing, and Byron held her ribbon. I was eight. They told me Switzerland. And they cut it in brass."

He coughed. Red on the snow.

"And the Crown knew. Every Lord Chancellor since sixteen eighty-nine has signed the annex and locked it in his desk. Vane signed it in my study, and asked me to spare him the details."

Then the thing he had never said. Not to anyone. Not in forty years.

"I could have gone down that stair myself. I raised a boy for it instead."

He pulled the wolf-crest ring off his finger, and pressed it into Nathaniel's palm, and closed his son's fingers over it.

"Don't be me. The lock opens with what closed it. Harding blood. This ring. And a blade that's already in it."

Nathaniel's other hand went behind him without looking, and found Alice's.

Eldgard Harding died.

Snow settled on his open eyes, and didn't melt.

* * *

Nathaniel stood up.

He didn't put the ring on. He put it in his pocket. From the same pocket, he took Jamie's key on its black ribbon, the one that opened Byron's old Guard stair.

Alice was at his side. Nixon was at the other.

They went in.

On the core stair, Crown troops were coming up. Tamara and Nixon held the landing against them, back to back, so the others could get down. Flash. Flash. The crack of rifles. Splinters of stone.

Under fire, Tamara said: "If we die, I want it on record I was married for twelve hours."

Nixon fired, reloaded. "Noted."

FZZT. FZZT.

* * *

Nathaniel and Alice reached the bottom of the core.

It was a vision of hell in green and white. The creature was screaming inside the Heartglass, which was cracked from top to bottom like a broken egg, green fire pouring through her. Morgana was straining at her hooks, roaring, the chain groaning. Lambrick and Vane were up on the gallery.

And at the great iron lever that controlled the Heartglass, with her black gloves on the handle, stood Josephine.

* * *

"Mother," said Nathaniel. "Step away."

"That's my sister," said Alice.

Josephine looked at them. Her face was very calm. She spoke gently, to neither of them.

"Your father said her mother would never allow it."

"Then don't," said Nathaniel.

"Four hundred years in hooks, Nathaniel. Nobody refuses the door."

It was the choice she had made at breakfast, weeks ago. Between her son and everything else. Made one last time, with her hand on iron.

If the girl held, no Harding would die tonight. And Josephine could live with what it had cost.

She pulled the lever. The iron shrieked in its housing, and the green fire roared up the glass.
