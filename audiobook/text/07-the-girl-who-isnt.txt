Episode Seven. The Girl Who Isn't.

Twenty-one days to first snow.

* * *

Gin Lane. The second of November. Morning.

Miriam's last day, from Miriam's side.

She woke before dawn, as she always did, and lay for a moment in the grey light listening to her sister breathe. Alice was asleep on the other side of the bed with her fists curled up under her chin, the way she had slept since she was a child. Twenty years old today. Miriam knew that. She had known it for a week.

She got up and was sick in the basin, quietly, so as not to wake her.

Then she stood at the window with one hand flat on her belly, and she smiled. She was radiant. She was terrified. She was leaving tonight.

She sat down at the little table with a pencil and a scrap of paper and began a letter.

Ali. About him. When we were small—

She stopped. She stared at the words for a long time. Out of the window, across the yard, she could see the fence. Eight posts.

She scored the line out. Then again, and again, harder, until the pencil tore the paper.

She started over on a fresh scrap, and this time she wrote fast, three short lines, without letting herself think. She folded it. She meant to leave it on Alice's pillow.

She looked at her sister's sleeping face. She lost her nerve.

She took down her long brown coat, slit the lining with her knife, and sewed the letter into it, small stitches, quick and neat.

Then she pulled on her red mittens, and went out.

* * *

Five weeks later, in a borrowed room on Gin Lane, Alice sat mending her sister's coat.

The long brown coat had been burned in the gasworks, the hem scorched, the lining torn. Alice sat by the window in the afternoon light with a needle and grey thread, and her needle caught on something in the lining. Something stiff.

She felt it with her fingers. Paper.

She cut the old stitches. She drew it out. Folded small. Miriam's handwriting.

I'm leaving with him. I'm sorry, Ali. Don't follow me.

She read it twice.

Miriam had been leaving. Miriam had never meant to say goodbye.

Then the door banged open and the creature came in from the market with her arms full of oranges, more oranges than two people could eat in a month, enormously proud of them, chattering about a man who sold hot chestnuts out of a drum, and how the drum had a fire inside it, and how he had let her hold one, a chestnut, and it was hot, and she had liked it.

She didn't use Alice's name. She hadn't since the gasworks.

Alice put the letter in her apron pocket. She smiled. She peeled the creature an orange.

She said nothing. She said nothing all evening. She sat across the table from the thing wearing her sister's face, and passed it bread, and wiped its chin, and said nothing at all.

* * *

Late that night, by one candle, Alice held out the letter.

"You were leaving."

The creature looked at it. She had lost Alice's name in the gasworks, but she still knew this face. She still knew this hurt.

"Yes."

And the fence came down. Because Alice was too angry to hold it up.

"You always left. You left me under that bed. Every time. You promised nothing would touch me, and then you counted fence posts while he—"

She couldn't finish.

Fifteen years, and she had finally said it out loud. To the only person who was there.

The creature had been waiting four weeks to be let in. She had the guilt. It was the last thing of Miriam's left in her, because the cold had taken the birthdays and the songs and the name, but it couldn't get its teeth into this.

She got down off the chair and knelt on the floor, so that she was lower than Alice. The way you'd kneel to talk to a child hiding under a bed.

"I should have stood in front of you," she said. "Every time. I'm sorry. I was so frightened of him that I let you be frightened alone. And I've hated myself every day since."

Then she said the thing Miriam never could have said.

"If he came through that door now, I'd stand in front of you. I'd let him freeze."

It was the apology Alice had wanted her whole life. Word for word. And it was perfect.

That was how she knew.

Miriam couldn't have said it. Miriam flinched.

Alice, barely a breath: "Say my name."

The creature opened her mouth.

There was nothing there.

A long silence. The candle hissed. Somewhere downstairs a door slammed.

"You're not her," said Alice.

"No."

Plainly. No more pretending. Morgana's daughter, who came through on the bridge, wearing Miriam's body and Miriam's memories like a borrowed coat.

Alice had known since the core that something else had come back in her sister. What she had built her whole theory to avoid knowing was whether Miriam was still under it.

"Is she in there? Under you?"

"No. She was gone before I opened her eyes. I'm sorry." A pause. "She was going to leave you, Ali. She told me, on the bridge. She was frightened. It wasn't of you."

Then the real horror, said gently, like a gift.

"I'm not sorry for her. I'm sorry for me. I love you. I don't know whose that is any more. I think it's mine."

Alice looked at the thing that had been her sister for four weeks. The thing that had remembered her birthday. That had peeled her an orange. That had just given her the only apology she had ever been given in her life.

And she understood that her real sister had never loved her this well.

"Get out."

The door closed.

* * *

Breakfast at Harding House was grey and quiet.

Eldgard sat at the head of the table, silent and diminished, his eggs untouched. Without his Works he looked like a coat with nobody inside it.

Josephine ran the table now. "The wedding is in seven days. The Lambrick money will buy the Works back."

Nathaniel put down his cup. "Or what, Mother? You'll poison me?"

Josephine buttered her toast. Scrape, scrape. "If I had to choose between you and London, I chose a long time ago."

Nathaniel stood. "If anything happens to the frost girl, I'll stand up in Parliament and say who poisoned Felix Hawke."

She didn't stop buttering. "Then it had better not happen from my hands."

* * *

In the core, under Crown control, Lord Chancellor Vane and Lord Lambrick took the tour.

Tamara came on her father's arm, in grey, saying nothing.

On the long stair down, Lambrick slowed, and spoke to her quietly. The only time he ever talked to her as if she were a person.

"Your mother hated the green. Said it made everyone at dinner look drowned. I kept her room on coal till the end."

Tamara looked at him.

Then they reached the bottom, and he looked up at Morgana, nine feet of ice in her hooks. There was no horror in his face. There was only arithmetic.

"She could be bled faster."

The man who had kept one room warm out of love would freeze a city for the same reason, and he called both of them providing.

On the clerk's desk at the foot of the stair lay Eldgard's seized folio of notes for the Heartglass. Tamara walked past it slowly. She read one line, upside down.

Vessel: White-born.

* * *

On Blackwood Bridge, in the fog, the creature walked to the place she first came through.

She stood at the rail, alone, where Miriam had stood, and did the only thing her body knew how to do with being sent away. She cried.

This time, the tears didn't freeze on her face. They froze the air.

Tink. Tink. Tink.

And then, krrrrrrk.

The thin place on the bridge, where she had stepped through from the White five weeks ago, cracked like a pane of glass. A jagged line of white light opened in the middle of the air.

Alice's "get out" had opened a door.

Howls in the fog. Long, low, rising.

Wolves came padding out of nothing. White-furred, blue-eyed, bigger than any wolf in any book. One, then three, then a dozen. They came out of the crack in the air, and they circled her, and then they lowered their great heads and bowed to her.

It was the most dangerous thing that could have happened.

* * *

On the roof of the Guard tower, the boyhood spot, two young men stood in the wind.

That morning, Nixon had been given a medal for carrying Cole out of the gasworks. The ceremony had been pure irony. Afterwards, Cole, two fingers short, had come up to him in the corridor and held out the diary.

"Today, sir."

So this afternoon, at the spot they had had since they were nine, Nixon told him.

"I fired the shot. I didn't know it was him."

Nathaniel hit him. Once, hard, in the face. Nixon's head snapped round. He let him.

"Your father told me to lie," Nixon said through the blood. "He came down after."

Nathaniel's voice was barely there. "Was Jamie alive when you left?"

"Yes."

Nathaniel walked away without another word.

A friendship of fifteen years ended on a roof.

* * *

In a gin shop on Gin Lane, a low room full of smoke and spit and broken chairs, Tamara Lambrick found Alice Bounds drunk.

She sat down across from her in her good grey coat, ignored the stares, and told her. Her father's arithmetic. She could be bled faster. And the line in the folio. Vessel: White-born.

Alice laughed, ugly. "Why would I care? She's not my sister."

Tamara looked at her. "She put out a fire around four children with the last of her cold, and forgot your name doing it. Did Miriam ever do that?"

Alice stared into her glass for a long time.

"No," she said. "Miriam never stood in front of anybody."

She stopped drinking.

* * *

The wolves that came through on Blackwood Bridge didn't stay there.

Where the chain was thinnest, over the old plague pits of Whitechapel, where the hanged Hargraves had been dumped four hundred years ago, the fog split open. And the wolves poured out into the gaslit streets.

Gas lamps froze and burst as they passed. Tink, tink, tink, all down the road. Bodies lay in the snowless street.

* * *

Nixon's Lanterns came.

Leapers on the rooftops, kshhh, THOOM. Flash-bursts, FZZT, and the wolves scattered from the light, yelping. Quench-blades of pale glass, cold as death.

It was black. Then a flash. A wolf, there. Black. A flash. Closer. Black. A flash. A wolf in mid-leap, jaws open, ten feet away.

Every flash showed them a wolf somewhere new, and nearer.

Nixon fought like a man trying to get himself killed. Cole fought beside him, holding his blade in a two-finger grip.

* * *

In the Pavilion music hall on Whitechapel Road, a hundred people were packed into the stalls, and the doors were barred.

On the stage, a comic singer in a checked suit and a red nose had them all singing. "Daisy, Daisy, give me your answer, do."

Outside, something was scratching at the doors. Skrrt. Skrrt.

They sang louder. "I'm half crazy, all for the love of you."

The scratching got louder.

They sang louder still.

A door panel bowed inward with a groan of wood. A little girl in the front row stopped singing. Her mother put an arm around her and started her again. "It won't be a stylish marriage."

Then, crash, through the skylight, Nathaniel Harding arrived on leap-boosters with a bag of flash-cartridges over his shoulder. And from the other side of the roof, crash, Nixon Hargrave came in after him.

They looked at each other. No words.

Then they fought back to back, the way they had at nine years old, perfectly in sync, one hour after the friendship ended.

* * *

At the stage door of the Pavilion, the creature walked into the street of wolves.

They turned toward her. All of them. A hundred blue eyes in the dark.

She said one word. Not English. A word in a language of wind, a long rising breath of sound.

They stopped. Every one of them.

Except the biggest. A huge grey-white beast, scarred across the muzzle, that kept coming.

She killed it with cold. It froze in mid-stride and fell over like a statue and shattered on the cobbles.

And she lost another memory.

Then she turned the rest. Every one. And she walked them back through the fog, a girl in a brown coat with a river of white wolves padding behind her, all the way to Blackwood Bridge. They went through the crack in the air one by one. When the last of them was gone, the crack closed behind it.

Tik.

A hundred people had watched a girl command wolves. By morning she would be the Frost Witch. The one creature in London who could open the White, and shut it again.

Then Alice, sober now, came running through the street of wolves to reach her.

The creature turned. She didn't know her face any more.

Alice stopped in front of her, out of breath.

"I'm Alice," she said. "I'm your sister."

Chosen. Not inherited.

* * *

At dawn, in Whitehall and in Shadwell, two buyers bid for one girl.

In his office, Lord Chancellor Vane declared martial law. The Frost Witch was to be captured.

Lambrick had read the folio. "Put her in the chain, and the Crown never needs a Harding again. The light becomes ours."

Vane heard only: the lights stay on.

Neither man said the other thing out loud. A girl who could open the White and shut it was worth more than the Works.

Across the river, in the steam of the Shadwell laundry, Josephine Harding and Mother Grise agreed a price for the Frost Witch, to be delivered to Josephine. Not to the Crown.

"Where is she?" Grise asked.

"Wherever my son puts her when the city gets too hot for her. Before Lantern Night that was my glasshouse, and after last night, it will be again. I want her taken by hands he can't trace to me."

She paused. "She's not a girl, Grise."

Grise poured the tea. "None of us were, by the time we came here."

* * *

All night, Nathaniel had fought beside the man who shot his brother. And one word from the roof had been going round in his head the whole time.

Yes.

Jamie had been alive when Nixon left.

At dawn he went to see his brother. Crowbar. Lantern. The Harding vault under St Bride's, down a stone stair slick with damp.

He broke the seal on the coffin his best friend had carried down these steps.

Krrk. CRACK.

Inside, laid out neatly in Jamie's burial coat, were three sacks of grey slag from the Works vats, and a family Bible, for weight.

No body.

Nathaniel stood in the candle-dark with a fistful of slag, and Morgana's voice from Lantern Night came back to him.

He is cold.

Above him, on the chancel wall, the brass plate still said: Died abroad.

* * *

Eldgard was drunk in his old gallery at the Works.

The vats stood dead all around him, their glass grey and empty. The Crown men hadn't come up here yet. He sat at a bench among them with a bottle.

Nathaniel walked in and dropped a fistful of slag on the bench in front of him. The same grey as every dead vat in the room.

"What did you do after Nixon left?"

"Ask me at the solstice."

"Now."

Eldgard looked at the slag for a long time.

"I finished it."

Then, almost to himself: "Your mother knew what this house spends. She found it out at my gate with a knife in her hand, and she married it anyway."

He didn't ask about the Heartglass. He didn't yet know there was anything to ask.

* * *

Alice brought the creature back into Harding House by the servants' door, and they crossed the long gallery of family portraits in the half dark.

The creature stopped at Jamie's.

Twenty-five years old, in oils, laughing at something outside the frame, a pair of skates slung over his shoulder.

Her hand went to her belly without her knowing it. Her breath caught on a feeling that wasn't hers. The whole of Miriam's love for him, arriving in her body all at once.

"What's wrong?" said Alice.

"Nothing."

Alice looked at the portrait. Then at her. She decided not to ask.

In the glasshouse, Nathaniel was waiting, the slag still grey in the creases of his hands. Alice didn't let him start.

"Tamara read me a line from your father's folio. Vessel: White-born." She stared at him. "You built a cage for my sister."

He hadn't known. She watched him not know.

And she hit him anyway. Harder than Nathaniel had hit Nixon.

Then: "So you know how to break it."

Before he could answer, the door opened, and Tamara came in out of the rain with the rest of what she'd read on her father's desk. A lunacy order. Two doctors' signatures. Waiting for the day she opened her mouth about his books.

"A spinster's nearest relative is her father," she said. "A wife's is her husband. The wedding is in seven days. I'd rather it were you than Bethlem."

Nathaniel looked at Alice.

Alice said, "Marry her."

"Bounds—"

"Marry her."

Both of them meant it. Both of them hated that they did.

Later, at the stove, Nathaniel told Alice about the coffin full of slag, and what Morgana had said on Lantern Night. Jamie isn't dead. He's cold.

Alice had lost her sister for good that very morning. "Don't live in it," she said. "I lived in it four weeks. It's warm. And then it isn't."

From the dark under the orange trees, the creature, who wasn't supposed to be listening, said quietly: "My mother doesn't lie."

Two answers. And Nathaniel could only believe one of them.

Twenty-one days. And everyone wanted her in the chain.

* * *

On a Gin Lane rooftop, under a sky with no stars, the creature asked Alice to teach her the counting game again. She had lost it.

And for the first time, Alice told her where it came from.

The fence outside the window. Eight posts. And he's done.

The creature listened. Then she counted them with her. One post. Two post. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight.

And at eight, she kept going.

"Nine."

"There's no nine," Alice said.

"There is now. Nine is the one where we get up."

Then she got it wrong on purpose. "Nine. Ten. Eleven. Fourteen. Twenty."

Alice laughed. It worked.

The game born under a bed had a new ending.

* * *

In the infirmary at the Guard barracks, Nixon was changing the dressings on Cole's hand when Tamara Lambrick came in.

She held out a folder of brown card, tied with string. Copied from her father's seized Harding papers.

"It has your name on it."

He took it.

Then, one week before her wedding to his best friend, she kissed him.

In the bed, Cole Pruett pretended very hard to be asleep.

Nixon didn't open the folder. He put it inside his coat, against his chest, and he sat by Cole's bed with his hand pressed over it, and he didn't sleep.
